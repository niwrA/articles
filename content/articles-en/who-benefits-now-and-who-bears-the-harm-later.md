A company can profit today from a decision whose real costs become visible only years later. Deferred maintenance may temporarily improve margins and subsequently cause failures. A product may grow rapidly while addiction risks, health harm or problematic debt emerge only after widespread adoption. Pollution may fall outside the enterprise and reach neighbours or future generations.

Decision-making power, personal benefit and consequences can therefore become separated. An executive may receive a bonus or sell shares before adverse effects appear. By the time repair is required, the company, a successor, shareholders or society carry the bill. The issue is not only how much risk an organisation can absorb financially, but also **who benefits, who can influence the outcome and who remains genuinely accountable later**.

This article builds on the linked article about safe payment. There, a concrete purchase amount can be reserved immediately. Here we ask what remains of the same principle when future harm is uncertain and cannot be expressed completely in money.

## From an exact amount to risk scenarios

For a consumer, €500 can literally be reserved. For a factory, bank or infrastructure project, future harm does not exist as one known amount. It depends on markets, failures, human behaviour, cumulative effects and rare events. Scaling up cannot therefore mean fully blocking every possible loss in advance. Nor is there one exact answer to how much capital a new platform may raise against an uncertain future return.

An enterprise can nevertheless compare exposure with its balance sheet, liquidity, buffers and ability to recover. Different scenarios can record how large harm might be, how likely it is, when it appears and who can bear it. The resulting risk profile may trigger reporting, additional buffers, closer supervision or prior approval.

For derivatives, DNB similarly considers stress tests, liquidity needs and buffers rather than notional positions alone.[1] The shared principle is that assumed risks should visibly reduce remaining capacity even when their exact outcome remains uncertain.

## What does not immediately appear on the balance sheet

### Future value, environmental harm and other hidden costs

A financially feasible project may still destroy long-term value. A choice producing €20 million of private profit and €30 million of harm elsewhere looks positive on a corporate balance sheet but not to society. Not every effect can be priced reliably either. Loss of health, biodiversity, autonomy or institutional trust quickly acquires a false precision when compressed into one monetary amount.

At least five questions are therefore needed alongside financial capacity: who benefits, who decides, who can influence the outcome, who bears the consequences and when do they appear? Separate scenarios and risk scales preserve those differences better than one moral aggregate score.

### Management rewards can separate time from responsibility

Salary, shares and options distribute upside and downside differently. An executive may be rewarded now for profit while maintenance, health or environmental risks appear after departure. Deferred compensation, clawback, personal liability and advance provisions can keep benefit and responsibility connected for longer.

This is not an argument for unlimited personal liability. Decision-makers need room to take responsible risks, and an adverse result does not prove that a decision was culpable. The relevant distinctions are influence, foreseeability, severity and culpability. An ordinary business setback, negligence, gross negligence and intent should not produce the same consequences.

### Severity alone does not determine prevention

A very severe sanction on paper prevents little when the probability of detection is almost zero. Even detected harm does not automatically produce personal consequences: it must be possible to attribute it to a decision and responsible people, after which an authorised party must actually act. Conversely, a moderate but credible consequence may influence conduct more than an extreme sanction that is almost never imposed.

The interactive model therefore separates four links: detection, attribution, enforcement and the concrete personal amount at risk. Culpability determines whether personal consequences are appropriate in the selected scenario at all. The preventive effect remains uncertain, so the model displays a range for weak, medium and strong behavioural responses.

## Regulating responsibility independently of form

Legislation aimed only at one company type, financial instrument or remuneration form can be avoided by repackaging risk. A broader duty of care can follow factual features: who has material decision-making power, who receives benefit, which adverse consequences are reasonably foreseeable and who can prevent, limit or repair them?

Such a rule does not make executives responsible for every imaginable future loss. A party that causes or materially contributes to a risk has a stronger duty than one remotely connected to it. A party without direct control but with substantial leverage may at least be required to investigate risks, set conditions and report outcomes.

Remuneration rules can follow function as well. The decisive question is not whether value is labelled a bonus, share or option, but whether someone receives early personal benefit from a decision whose material consequences appear later. An appropriate part of that reward can remain connected to the consequences through deferral, clawback or liability for culpable conduct.

Combining a general duty with sector-specific detail avoids two extremes. Detailed product rules age quickly; open standards alone make outcomes unpredictable. Statutory principles, concrete thresholds and scenario requirements in secondary rules, independent review and periodic revision can respond faster to new constructions without abandoning legal protection.

## Two applications

### Pay later

A deferred-payment provider is not responsible for every individual purchase, but it is responsible for foreseeable effects of its own product design at scale. It sets limits, reminders and interfaces, earns from use and may see patterns no individual shop can see. The AFM expects financial firms to consider target markets, performance in different scenarios, customer interests and review during product development.[2] OECD guidance similarly asks enterprises to identify and limit adverse impacts from their products and services.[3]

The relevant question is not whether one pair of shoes was morally permissible, but whether the system foreseeably promotes debt stacking, impulsive use or harm among vulnerable groups and how the provider responds. Here societal responsibility meets the concrete design of a financial product.

### Hedging and speculation

A put option is not wrong because it rises when a share falls; it may limit an existing risk. The analysis changes when a party profits greatly from harm it can influence. The underlying position, decision-making power, possible harm and accountability must then be considered together. The same instrument may provide useful protection or create a perverse incentive depending on the relationship between benefit, power and consequences.

## What could limit or refute the framework?

The model must add something demonstrable beyond existing accounting, supervision and liability. This can be tested by stating in advance which situations it identifies as risky and comparing those predictions with independent case analyses. A useful framework should, for example, flag greater risk when early personal reward coincides with large influenceable harm, little chance of detection and consequences that are barely recoverable.

If those predictions bear no relationship to later harm, or if a simpler combination of existing stress tests, reporting and liability rules distinguishes the cases better, the framework should be revised or rejected. Counterproductive behaviour also counts: excessive personal threat may reduce openness, encourage defensive decisions or deter suitable executives.

## Conclusion

At societal scale, an exactly reserved amount gives way to scenarios, risk ranges and buffers. This reveals a second problem: profit, decision-making power and personal reward may arise early while financial, social or environmental consequences reach others much later.

Future-proof design should therefore show more than how much risk an organisation can bear. It should reveal where consequences land, who could influence them and through which successive links responsible decision-makers can still be held to account. Rules should follow actual power, benefit, foreseeability and culpability — not merely legal packaging.

I can read this sentence silently. I can also make my own voice read it aloud in my head, or deliberately use another voice. I can understand the words faster than that imagined speaker pronounces them, and I can stop the narration again. For me, then, an inner voice is one possible form of thinking rather than thought itself.

That ordinary variation does not make a much harder phenomenon simple, but it opens a useful question. What changes when inner language does not begin voluntarily, does not stop when a person wants it to, or is not experienced as one's own thought? Can our model of consciousness and self say anything useful about that?

The question arose while I watched part of Maasja Ooms's documentary *Mijn woord tegen het mijne*. It follows five people who hear voices and a psychiatrist who attempts to speak not only about but also with the voices. The film shows what such a conversation can mean to people. A documentary is not a controlled study, but it can reveal experiences that a theory ought to describe.[1]

This article therefore does not extend the general theory as though voice-hearing simply followed from it. It is an **application and stress test**. If the model is useful, it should distinguish among the content of a thought, its access to consciousness, and the sense that the thought was produced by and belongs to me.

### Experience before diagnosis

Hearing voices is not synonymous with schizophrenia. Auditory verbal hallucinations occur across psychiatric diagnoses, in neurological and physical conditions, around sleep, after trauma and among people without a clinical diagnosis. Experiences also differ substantially: one voice or many, speech or other sounds, friendly, neutral or threatening, recognised or unknown, occasional or almost continuous.[2]

Another term that can arise in discussions of multiplicity is **dissociative identity disorder (DID)**. Official clinical descriptions associate DID with discontinuity of identity and gaps in memory beyond ordinary forgetting.[15] That connects directly to dimensions examined in this article, but DID is not synonymous with voice-hearing, plural internal deliberation or one particular organisation of consciousness. It is therefore relevant context for the stress test, but not a preset to which the interactive model reduces a diagnosis.

That diversity warns against one grand explanation. Even when two people both hear a voice, the decisive mechanism need not be the same. One case may be closely related to trauma and dissociation, another to perceptual expectations, speech production, sleep deprivation, substances, or a combination of vulnerability and circumstance.[3]

A good model should therefore not ask for *the* cause of voice-hearing. It should ask which processes interact differently in a particular case and what predictions follow.

### Inner speech is not a single channel

Research on inner speech shows that ‘a voice in your head’ is not a uniform human default. Inner language can be condensed or fully elaborated, dialogic or monologic, deliberately evoked or spontaneous, and more or less voice-like. Not all thought is verbal: imagery, spatial relations, dispositions to act and feelings can carry information without an inner narrator.[4]

This fits the earlier hypothesis that consciousness mostly works with summaries of largely unconscious processing. A sentence may arrive without our having access to the systems that selected its words, activated memories and made the outcome relevant. Ordinarily, such an outcome is incorporated into a working self-model: *I am thinking this*, even though I do not know how I produced the thought.

But ‘consciously available’ and ‘made by me’ are not equivalent. We can distinguish at least four properties:

1. **Content:** which words, images or intentions appear?
2. **Access:** does that information enter conscious focus?
3. **Ownership:** does the experience feel like mine?
4. **Agency:** does it feel as though I started, directed or can stop it?

These properties usually travel together in daily life. Exceptions reveal that they are different functions. An unexpected thought can clearly feel mine without being deliberately chosen. A familiar sentence may feel alien or inserted. Accounts of auditory verbal hallucination therefore connect inner-speech processes to altered source and self-monitoring, among other mechanisms, but no existing model explains the full variety.[5]

### The self as organisation, not a little person

The base article proposed treating the ‘I’ as an organising model. The brain must evaluate diverse signals and goals in relation to one organism: this body, this history, these capacities and consequences. That requires no indivisible controller inside the head. A workable sense of self may arise because information is usually marked consistently as belonging to this organism, caused by this organism and relevant to this organism.

The simplest application to voice-hearing remains that a process may produce content that gains conscious access but is insufficiently connected to the ordinary sense of ownership and agency. The content is present and its production remains hidden, as usual; only the customary attribution *I am making this* is absent or points elsewhere. If this pattern recurs, recognisable language, emotion and subject matter may give the experience a more stable identity.

That account still assumes that behind the altered attribution lies one largely coherent self-model. Compartmentalisation is a deeper possibility. The organisation that binds memories, emotions, goals and possibilities for action into one continuing ‘I’ may itself contain boundaries of varying permeability.

### Information boundaries within one experienced self

Such boundaries are not inherently abnormal. People behave differently with parents, friends, partners, at work or while pursuing a hobby, while continuing to experience themselves as the same person. Each setting activates different expectations, memories, skills and social rules. A completely context-insensitive self would be poorly adapted.

The relevant difference is between **flexible specialisation** and **rigid information boundaries**. With flexible specialisation, information from other contexts remains available when needed. With stronger compartmentalisation, the current situation or emotional state determines which memories, feelings, goals and responses become accessible enough to influence arbitration. Research on autobiographical memory shows that emotion affects both how experiences are stored and which memories are later retrieved. That is not evidence of compartmentalisation by itself, but it does demonstrate that access to memory can be state-dependent.[9]

Temporarily separating fear, pain or an irresolvable conflict may be protective, allowing someone to continue functioning elsewhere. The same solution can later become restrictive when relevant knowledge no longer transfers between contexts, emotions appear without a recognisable cause, or a protective response remains active after its original circumstances have gone.

This connects directly to the arbitration model. Conscious arbitration can use only information that is currently available and sufficiently relevant. A person may experience themselves as the same conscious individual while danger, painful memories and defensive responses dominate in a threatening state, and trust, approach and other memories participate in a safe state. Behaviour can be intelligible within either state yet appear alien or contradictory from the other.

The self-model is therefore not only the reference point *for which* information is evaluated. It also helps determine **which parts of the organism and its history are admitted to that evaluation**.

### From schema modes to more autonomous positions

Schema therapy offers a useful intermediate case. It describes *modes*: temporarily activated combinations of emotion, belief, bodily response and coping strategy. A Vulnerable Child, an angry or detached protector and a punitive critic can select quite different information and produce quite different behaviour in response to the same situation. People will usually continue to experience these states as belonging to the same self, even when they can barely recognise a reaction afterwards.

Such a mode may have served a useful function. Withdrawal, numbing, control or anger may have helped someone endure circumstances in which open feeling, trust or resistance was unsafe. The strategy may later be applied too broadly, too quickly or too rigidly. Recognising what it once accomplished can make it possible to appreciate its protection without treating every current response as necessary. This functional perspective is important in schema therapy, but the precise origin and activation of modes has received only limited experimental study.[14]

This does not establish that voices are autonomous schema modes. The similarity does, however, suggest a possible continuity. As ownership, memory, mutual information access and influence over action diverge, a state may be experienced less as *me in a particular mode* and more as a recognisable other position, voice or part. This is not a linear developmental ladder: someone may have several clearly distinguished voices while sharing extensive memory and cooperation, whereas another person without pronounced voices may have strong state-dependent differences in memory or behaviour.

| Dimension | Strongly shared self-state | More autonomous voice or position |
|---|---|---|
| Ownership | *I am reacting this way now* | *This does not come from me* or *this is another part* |
| Perspective | Temporary pattern | Recognisable and relatively durable standpoint |
| Communication | Self-reflection within one self | Dialogue between distinguished positions |
| Memory | Largely shared | Shared, asymmetric or partly sealed off |
| Influence on action | One experienced decision-maker | Advice, competition, veto or temporary takeover |
| Identity | One individual in different states | Individual, group or changing coalition |

### Neurotransmitters change the field of forces

So far, this may sound mainly like a description of information processing. Yet that organisation does not exist independently of the brain's biological state. Neurotransmitters do not each carry a particular thought, emotion or voice, nor do they function as independent sliders. Across different locations and timescales, they modulate excitability, learning, attention, inhibition and the significance assigned to signals. Their effects depend on receptor, brain region, network state and interactions with other systems.[11]

Dopamine is particularly relevant to psychosis, but the familiar summary ‘too much dopamine’ is too crude. Research points instead to dysregulation in particular dopaminergic circuits. One influential account proposes that events, associations or internally generated signals may consequently acquire unusual weight or *salience*. Glutamate and GABA contribute to excitation, inhibition and coordination between networks; serotonin, noradrenaline and acetylcholine influence flexibility, arousal, attention and the weighting of expectations against sensory information. These systems overlap, and none independently explains the diversity of voice-hearing or schizophrenia-spectrum disorders.[12]

Within this model, these substances therefore alter the **field of forces** in which processes compete. An internally generated sentence may demand more attention; source recognition may become less reliable; threat may gain faster access to behaviour; or conscious evaluation may occur without gaining sufficient influence over inhibition and execution. Stress hormones, sleep deprivation, physical illness and substance use may temporarily alter the same effective capacities. The percentages in the interactive model should therefore not be read as fixed properties of separate brain regions or of a person, but as state-dependent outcomes of a dynamic system.

This also helps explain why biological and psychological interventions need not represent competing explanations. Medication may reduce the salience, conviction or compelling quality of experiences without fully changing their autobiographical meaning or the information boundaries between self-positions. Dialogue, learning and restoration of sleep or safety may affect other parts of the same system. This remains a working hypothesis: a therapeutic effect does not by itself reveal the mechanism that originally produced an experience.

### From context-sensitivity to multiple self-positions

As information boundaries harden, several configurations become possible. These are neither diagnoses nor fixed stages of a single developmental path. They describe which functions may be shared or separated at a particular time:

| Configuration | Self-organisation | Conscious access | Memory and control | Meaning for the model |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Context-sensitive self | One largely coherent self-model | Broadly shared | Information remains transferable | Already fits the model |
| Unowned content | One self-model with incomplete self-attribution | The thought is consciously available | Memory mostly shared; little control over content | Requires an explicit distinction between content, ownership and agency |
| Simultaneous self-positions | Several partial perspectives | Positions can experience and answer one another | Memories overlap; proposals compete | Requires multiple local self-models within shared arbitration |
| Alternating control | Several state-dependent organisations | One position gains broad access at a time | Memory may be asymmetric; control shifts | Requires access gates and state transitions |
| Parallel organisation | Strongly separated information and control channels | May be partly parallel | Perception or action can be divided by channel | A boundary test for the model's unity |

In voice-hearing, a partly separated perspective may acquire recognisable language, emotion, intention or identity, becoming almost perceptible as an interlocutor. In a lighter configuration, only content appears without automatic ownership. In a stronger configuration, local self-positions may carry their own memories and goals, communicate with one another or compete for attention and behaviour. At times, one organisation may temporarily gain broad access to final control.

Reports by voice-hearers are more varied still than a succession of one voice and one listening self. In a phenomenological study of 153 participants, 81% reported multiple voices; voices could have characterful properties, converse and change over time.[13] Other descriptions include several positions being present simultaneously, deliberating with one another, having different levels of awareness of events, communicating asymmetrically and exerting different degrees of influence over execution. Functionally, this resembles a vehicle in which one position drives, another actively advises or intervenes from the passenger seat, some positions converse from the back seat, others receive only part of the information and still others gain access only after the journey. The metaphor is not a neurological map, but it shows why conscious presence, communication, memory, arbitration and action must be represented as separate properties.

This does not demonstrate that several complete consciousnesses literally exist. Even research on people whose cerebral hemispheres were surgically disconnected finds a mixture of separated processing and remaining unity. Current evidence does not settle whether one or more subjective consciousnesses result.[10] For this model, it is therefore more precise to begin with partly autonomous **self-organisations**.

### What fits, and what must change?

The lighter configurations already fit the existing model. Specialised processes can make competing proposals, a thought can become conscious without being voluntarily selected, and ownership need not be identical to access. The extension begins where not only information but also the reference point of arbitration becomes variable.

The model must then represent the self as a temporary coalition of accessible memories, a model of body and perspective, goals, values, ownership and possibilities for action. These elements ordinarily overlap enough to function as one continuing ‘I’. Compartmentalisation may create different coalitions using partly different information.

The functional ‘last word’ also acquires a more precise meaning. One body must ultimately reduce many actions to one executable trajectory, but the coalition controlling attention, further processing or behaviour need not always be the same. Several positions may be conscious and deliberate locally at the same time, while one directly drives, a second advises or intervenes and others exert only indirect influence. Arbitration then becomes not one circle but a network of local evaluations around a shared execution gateway. The model remains useful, but neither its self-model nor its arbitration can continue to be assumed singular and fully integrated.

We must also distinguish the outcome of arbitration from its execution. Conscious consideration may produce a preference or decision without that outcome gaining sufficient access to motor, emotional or inhibitory processes. Conversely, a rapid automatic response may already be underway before conscious arbitration can redirect it. Even doing nothing is not always a freely selected outcome: it may reflect delay, deliberate inhibition, freezing, or a blockage between decision and execution. The interactive model therefore treats this execution coupling as a separate variable.

### Integration without explaining the experience away

If a voice is partly maintained because its content is organised as unknown, external or absolutely separate, integration need not begin by saying: *you are the same person*. That is a theoretical conclusion that can deny lived experience. It may also bypass the genuine fear, meaning or memory associated with a voice.

A subtler route is to make the information boundary less absolute through acquaintance. When does the voice appear? What does it respond to? Which words, concerns and power relationships recur? Which memories, emotions and possibilities for action become accessible in that state, and which do not? Mapping such relationships may connect what felt wholly foreign to context and life history.

Integration need not mean that positions disappear, merge or adopt one identity. It may instead consist of mutual recognition, better transmission of information and the development of a procedure for making decisions. Some people describe cooperation becoming possible only after different parts could acknowledge that each had tried, in its own way, to survive difficult circumstances. Beginning with small, safe joint decisions can then provide practice in listening, turn-taking, compromise and trust. This is not a universal treatment protocol, but it demonstrates an important theoretical point: a stable organisation may also be more democratic and durably plural.

The *Healthy Adult* of schema therapy need not be treated as one privileged internal person within such an organisation. It can instead denote a distributed function: distinguishing present circumstances from past danger, hearing different interests, setting boundaries, caring for the whole and reaching executable agreements. In a self experienced as unitary, this may feel like one reflective ‘I’; in a self experienced as a group, the same regulatory function may reside in rules, deliberation or a changing coalition.

This idea overlaps with experimental therapies. *Talking With Voices* studies structured dialogue with voices within therapy; early trials have concentrated on feasibility and possible mechanisms.[6] In AVATAR therapy, therapist and participant give a distressing voice a face and sound, then use controlled dialogue to practise a different relationship with it. A randomised trial found that two variants reduced voice severity more than the control treatment after sixteen weeks, although differences were no longer clear at 28 weeks.[7]

These studies do not prove that ‘parts of the self merge again’. Dialogue might also work by reducing fear, increasing control, changing expectations and attention, or weakening a submissive relationship to the voice. That is exactly why this makes an interesting test: different explanations predict partly different changes.

### What changes should this model predict?

The proposed explanation is still broad enough to be made compatible with quite different outcomes after the fact. To investigate it properly, we should specify in advance what the compartmentalisation and integration account in particular predicts will change. Less fear or distress from voices is not distinctive enough: a changed power relationship, expectation, attention or general therapeutic support could produce the same outcome.

If information boundaries are central, more specific predictions follow:

- The stronger the information boundaries, the more behaviour, self-description and memory should differ between emotional or social contexts.
- People, places, bodily signals or emotions may act as access keys to particular memories, self-positions or voices.
- Sleep deprivation, stress and pharmacological changes should be able to alter not only the frequency of experiences but, measurably and potentially independently, their salience, source recognition, grip on attention or access to execution.
- In at least some cases, voice content and identity should be systematically connected to particular emotions, relationships, memories, goals or protective strategies.
- With simultaneous self-positions, strong dominance of one position should temporarily reduce the influence or accessibility of conflicting goals.
- With alternating control, the current state should predict which information becomes accessible and which position exerts most influence over behaviour.
- If dialogue helps through integration, mutual knowledge, autobiographical coherence and recognition of shared emotions or intentions should increase.
- Improvement need not remove voices or contextual differences; it should primarily increase the freedom to carry relevant information and possibilities for action between states.

The final two predictions make the mechanism more discriminating. If distress falls without a change in information overlap or mutual predictability, the intervention may still be valuable but would not specifically support the integration account. If permeability increases before competition and distress decline, that fits the proposed developmental sequence better. That sequence too may be wrong: perhaps control generally precedes autobiographical coherence, or reduced fear proves sufficient without further integration.

Research involving people who can evoke or influence voices does indicate that control is not all-or-nothing and that greater control often accompanies less distress.[8] This does not establish cause and effect. Perhaps control reduces distress; perhaps lower distress enables control; both directions probably contribute.

### Not uniformity, but greater shared freedom

This model does not determine which treatment suits an individual; that requires professional assessment, safety and personal context. It does suggest that plurality or contextual variation is not automatically the problem itself. Distress, danger, loss of control, disrupted sleep and the ability to function in daily life matter more.

It may therefore be useful first to map which states activate a voice, what information is available to different positions, whether they communicate simultaneously or alternate, and what protective function a response might serve. For one person, recognising inner processes may matter; for another, a less fearful relationship with a stable voice; with strong compartmentalisation, greater transferability of memories, emotions and practical agreements may be more important.

The goal of integration need not be one uniform personality behaving identically everywhere. A healthy self remains context-sensitive. Here, integration primarily means **permeability**: relevant information can cross boundaries when needed, different positions become more predictable to one another, and the person gains more freedom in how the whole system responds.

This gives us a better research question than choosing between ‘the voice really is another person’ and ‘the voice does not exist’. The experience is real. The open questions concern how content, source, meaning, memory and behavioural control are organised, which boundaries remain protective and which now mainly restrict freedom.

After COVID-19, many debates have examined which measures worked, arrived too late or caused more harm than expected. That evaluation is necessary, but it can also trap preparedness inside the previous crisis. The next serious outbreak need not involve a respiratory virus, transmission before symptoms or the same pressure on hospitals. A plan that chiefly remembers measures therefore learns less than a system that remembers **why** a measure made sense under particular conditions.

The better opening question is not *what did we do last time?* It is: **which characteristics of this pathogen, in this situation, make which interventions effective and proportionate?** This is no longer merely a thought experiment. In 2025, the WHO published a threat-agnostic Decision Navigator combining epidemiology, health-system capacity, medical countermeasures, resources, feasibility, acceptance and unintended consequences.[1] The framework proposed here makes the same shift concrete and auditable.

## The same category can hide different systems

Ebola, a novel respiratory virus and Q fever are all infectious diseases, yet they do not call for the same toolbox. Ebola spreads primarily through direct contact with blood and other body fluids from ill or deceased people; according to the WHO, people do not transmit it before symptoms begin. Rapid recognition, isolation, protective practice, contact tracing, safe clinical care and safe burials therefore form a coherent package.[2]

A respiratory virus with substantial transmission around or before symptom onset presents a different problem. Research early in the COVID-19 pandemic found that infectiousness could already be substantial before symptoms appeared.[3] Symptom screening and isolation of known cases retain value, but cannot serve as the only defence. Ventilation, filtration, respiratory protection, broad surveillance and rapid diagnostics become more important.

Q fever changes the system again. *Coxiella burnetii* occurs in animals including goats, sheep and cattle; people can be infected by inhaling dust contaminated with animal excretions or birth products.[4] Humans are then not necessarily the main link at which policy should intervene. Veterinary surveillance, occupational protection, farm controls and environmental monitoring may matter more than general restrictions on contact between people.

These examples establish no rigid one-to-one rule. Routes can coexist and interventions work in packages. They do show why a universal pandemic checklist commits a category error: one label—infectious disease—conceals different causal structures.

## Profile the disease and situation first

A decision system begins with a standard profile. It records dominant and possible transmission routes, pathogen stability in air, water, surfaces or dust, and what is known about the infectious dose. It then describes timing: incubation period, latent period, infectious period, peak infectiousness, and transmission without or before symptoms.

These concepts should not be collapsed. Incubation runs from infection to symptoms; latency from infection to infectiousness. A long incubation period gives contact tracing ample time only if infectiousness does not start much earlier. Once people transmit well before recognisable illness, retrospective case finding may move more slowly than the chains it is trying to catch.

Spread, severity and healthcare demand follow. (R_0) conditionally describes average secondary infections in a fully susceptible population, but does not by itself reveal clinical severity or how unevenly transmission is distributed. A modest individual probability of intensive care can still exceed capacity when many infections occur together. Nor is capacity simply a bed count: staffing, oxygen, diagnostics, protective equipment, medication and length of stay can each become the binding constraint.

Finally, the profile contains population immunity, diagnostics, vaccines, treatments, vulnerable groups and uncertainty. ‘Unknown’ is not an empty cell to be silently filled with an average. It is information about decision risk and should change monitoring, precaution and research priorities.

## From profile to package, not a single winner

Beside the disease profile sits an intervention database. Each intervention records its mechanism, relevant transmission routes, speed, cost, required infrastructure, strength of evidence, distributional effects and reversibility. The WHO classifies public-health and social measures into areas including active case finding and contact identification, personal protection, environmental measures, social measures and international travel or trade measures.[5] Clinical care, vaccination, source control and healthcare expansion complete the toolbox.

This changes the language of debate. ‘Do masks work?’ is too coarse. The relevant question is how much respiratory protection adds in a given exposure setting, considering transmission route, product quality, fit, adherence and alternatives. ‘Should schools close?’ becomes: what health benefit do we expect, what educational and distributional effects follow, how uncertain are both, and is there a less burdensome package with similar benefit?

The answer is usually not one isolated measure. Isolation without income support may be effective on paper and infeasible in practice. Testing without a rapid result arrives too late. Ventilation without maintenance deteriorates. A vaccine may mainly prevent severe illness, mainly reduce transmission, do both, or do neither sufficiently against a new variant. A serious model must therefore compare bundles and represent interactions, implementation and reach.

## Facts do not calculate values

Ranking packages requires objectives. Reduced transmission, mortality and pressure on healthcare are benefits. Financial cost, learning loss, psychosocial harm, inequality and restrictions on rights are possible burdens. Speed, scalability and public acceptance determine whether a theoretically effective measure produces its expected effect.

Science can estimate these consequences; epidemiological data cannot determine how much weight society *ought* to give each one. Two people can accept the same estimates yet prefer different packages because they value mortality risk, education, liberty or distribution differently. That disagreement is not automatically science denial. It becomes problematic when a value judgement is presented as a law of nature, or a factual claim as merely a preference.

A useful system therefore stores both, without merging them: the inputs and evidential confidence of factual estimates, and the explicit weights and constraints of the decision-maker. Calculation can support a choice but cannot replace democratic legitimacy.

## Uncertainty changes the question

Point estimates are often falsely precise early in an outbreak. Severity may be biased upwards because mild infections remain undetected; hospital ratios lag behind transmission; an effect estimate from another disease or society may not transfer. A mature model therefore stores ranges, data sources, dates and the quality of every assumption.

It can then evaluate thousands of plausible combinations. The interesting output is not only which package ranks highest on average, but how often it remains attractive under reasonable uncertainty. A cheap, reversible measure that helps in nearly every scenario may be more defensible than a burdensome measure that wins only under one narrow estimate.

Uncertainty analysis can also produce a research agenda. If the policy ranking barely changes when the incubation period becomes more precise, further work on that parameter has little immediate decision value. If the choice reverses with the proportion of pre-symptomatic transmission, that parameter has high *value of information*. Research is then prioritised not simply by what is unknown, but by which unknown could alter action.

## Decide in advance what should change the decision

Advice should not be an endpoint. It should contain indicators, thresholds, a review date and predefined reasons for escalation or relaxation. These might combine (R_t), hospital admissions, projected intensive-care occupancy, staff absence, test positivity and wastewater signals. No single indicator represents the entire system; together they can show whether the situation for which a package was chosen still exists.

This cannot remove political conflict, but it makes decisions more auditable. On Monday, a measure may be reasonable under deep uncertainty and threatened healthcare failure; three weeks later, better evidence, treatment or lower severity can make the same measure disproportionate. Changing advice then need not show that science failed. It can demonstrate that the system processed new evidence.

Versioning is essential. Every decision should retain what was known, which sources and uncertainties were used, which objectives were weighted, which alternatives were considered, and why advice later changed. This creates an inspectable memory rather than a story reconstructed after the event.

## The model must be able to contradict itself

The strictest design criterion is simple: provide different facts and the system must be able to reach a different conclusion. Without pre-symptomatic transmission and with traceable contacts, targeted tracing should rise. With an animal source, source control should dominate. Under severe healthcare pressure, temporary capacity protection should gain weight. Once an effective vaccine or treatment becomes available, some non-medical burdens should be capable of falling.

A system that recommends the same package for every pathogen is not a decision model but a preference with an interface. The opposite failure is equally dangerous: a score that presents an exact optimum without calibrated inputs pretends to knowledge that does not exist. The most useful result may therefore be that several packages are approximately equivalent, that none is convincing, or that one particular unknown should be investigated first.

Preparedness does not require predicting which pathogen will emerge. It requires infrastructure that can rapidly construct a profile, compare interventions proportionately, track uncertainty, prioritise research and revise decisions transparently. The next pandemic may require familiar measures—or their exact opposite. A good system need not know that in advance. It must recognise the difference in time.

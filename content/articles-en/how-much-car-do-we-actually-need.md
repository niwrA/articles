There is no single correct answer to the question of car size. A family travelling with four people every day, a contractor carrying tools and a trailer, and a city dweller who usually drives alone have different functional requirements. The relevant question is therefore not whether a small or large car is generally better, but how much capacity someone needs routinely and how much is purchased only for exceptions.

## Car obesity

The Dutch satirist Arjen Lubach once devoted a segment to what he called *autobesitas*: car obesity. The issue remains relevant whenever the future of mobility is discussed. That discussion usually centres on two questions: how much should we drive, and what should power our vehicles? The second has received particular attention because of electrification. A third variable is still discussed too little: the size of the vehicle itself.

The figures show why this remains necessary. According to Statistics Netherlands (CBS), a Dutch passenger car built in 2024 weighed an average of 1,554 kilograms, compared with 1,224 kilograms for a 2016 model. Average length increased from 4.20 to 4.41 metres and width from 1.76 to 1.82 metres. Across eight model years, the average car therefore gained 330 kilograms, 21 centimetres in length and 6 centimetres in width. A quarter of all Dutch passenger-car kilometres are now driven in cars weighing more than 1,500 kilograms; in 2018 that share was 15%.[1]

Part of the weight increase has a straightforward explanation. Electric cars are relatively heavy because of their batteries, while electrification is desirable for several reasons. But batteries do not explain the full development, still less why cars have simultaneously become longer, wider and often taller. SUVs and crossovers now account for a growing share of the market.

The resulting question is whether the benefits—more interior and luggage space, often greater towing capacity, easier entry and, for some users, greater comfort—outweigh the disadvantages. To answer it, we need to examine several dimensions of vehicle size.

### Mass behaviour

Safety is one of the most attractive properties of a large, heavy car, and it has a real physical basis. In a collision between a heavy and a light vehicle, the mass ratio favours the occupants of the heavier one.

A highly simplified calculation illustrates the effect. If two 1,200-kilogram cars collide head-on at 50 km/h each, their changes in velocity are approximately equal. The same applies to two 2,000-kilogram cars. But if a 2,000-kilogram car meets a 1,200-kilogram car at the same speed, a simple perfectly inelastic model produces changes in velocity of approximately 37.5 km/h for the heavy car and 62.5 km/h for the light car.

Real crashes are much more complicated. Crumple zones, body structure, overlap, airbags, seatbelts and collision angle strongly affect the outcome. The mass effect nevertheless remains. Dutch research by [**Van Ommeren, Rietveld, Zagha Hop and Sabir**](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2212012213000142) found that an additional 500 kilograms in the other car was associated with an approximately 70% increase in fatality risk relative to the mean risk and a roughly 30% increase in serious-injury risk.

A large Belgian analysis by [**VIAS**](https://vias.be/publications/Impact%20voertuigkenmerken/Impact_van_voertuigkenmerken.pdf) shows the same asymmetry. In its statistical model, an additional 300 kilograms reduced the fatality risk of a vehicle's own occupants by 48%, while increasing the risk for occupants of the other car by 77%. Fatality risk for vulnerable road users increased by 28%. These are statistical associations in crash data rather than predictions for every individual collision, but the general pattern is clear.

That is precisely where the collective problem arises. If most other cars weigh 1,200 kilograms, buying a 2,000-kilogram vehicle may be rational from the perspective of my own safety. If everyone follows the same reasoning, two-tonne cars once again collide with two-tonne cars. Part of the original advantage was positional: I was safer because I was heavier than the other party, not simply because my car was heavy in absolute terms.

Road-safety literature describes this as a *vehicle arms race*. Scaling up may be individually rational while producing little collectively beyond a heavier fleet. Cyclists and pedestrians are particularly disadvantaged because they cannot participate in the race.[4][5]

Mass also matters when nothing collides. A heavier car generally requires more material to manufacture and more energy to move. Tyres and infrastructure are more heavily loaded as well. The effect on structural road damage should not be exaggerated: axle load is particularly important, which makes heavy lorries many times more consequential per vehicle than passenger cars. The classic *fourth-power law* illustrates how rapidly damage can rise with axle load, although the exponent is not a universal law for every road and damage mechanism. Differences between light and heavy passenger cars are not the main cause of motorway wear, but ever more weight does not help.[6]

### A matter of perspective

Tall vehicles are popular for an understandable reason: many drivers enjoy the elevated seating position and perceive a better overview. While surrounding vehicles remain lower, that may indeed be an advantage. Yet this is the same positional dynamic as mass. When more drivers sit higher, the relative view advantage disappears, while visibility of the wider environment deteriorates.

Tall vehicles have themselves become larger visual obstructions. A high car ahead blocks the view through traffic, and a tall parked vehicle can more easily hide a cyclist or a child crossing the road. A higher seating position also does not automatically provide better visibility close to the vehicle itself.

Height carries an efficiency cost. A tall body may help with access, headroom or ground clearance, but each extra centimetre of external height does not automatically create the same amount of usable interior space. Aerodynamic drag depends not only on the drag coefficient but also on frontal area. A tall and wide vehicle therefore presents a larger design challenge.

According to the International Energy Agency, the sales-weighted average electricity consumption of electric SUVs in 2022 was approximately 20% higher than that of other electric cars. Height alone does not explain this difference: mass, width, tyres and design also contribute. It nevertheless demonstrates that size remains energetically relevant within an electric fleet.[2]

For cyclists and pedestrians, the height of the vehicle front is especially important. In the Belgian VIAS analysis, an additional 10 centimetres of bonnet height was associated with a 27% increase in fatality risk for vulnerable road users. A meaningful definition of vehicle safety should therefore consider not only how well a vehicle protects its occupants, but also the risks it imposes on others.

### Give me space

Space may be the most tangible argument. A Dutch street does not become wider when the vehicles parked in it do. The average external dimensions of a 2016 car were approximately 4.20 by 1.76 metres, according to CBS. Treated as a simple rectangle, that is 7.39 m². For a 2024 car, 4.41 by 1.82 metres amounts to approximately 8.03 m²: an increase of about 8.6%.

Parking makes the effect clearer still. In a 2.30-metre-wide space, a 1.76-metre car leaves a total margin of 54 centimetres. A 1.82-metre car leaves 48 centimetres. A few per cent of additional vehicle width therefore removes more than 11% of the remaining margin. That is noticeable when opening doors, using car parks and navigating older residential streets.

Compactness is not only a social benefit. For someone who regularly parks in a Dutch city, a shorter and narrower car may simply be a better product. It fits in more places, manoeuvres more easily and leaves more space around it. The luggage capacity of a larger model may be valuable for three weeks a year, while twenty centimetres less length makes every parking operation easier during the remaining 49 weeks.

The spatial effect does not end once a car begins moving. A longer vehicle occupies more road length, although at normal motorway speeds this is small relative to safe following distance. Near capacity, it becomes more relevant. Congestion often begins at bottlenecks such as merges and weaving sections, where vehicle length, headways and lane changes jointly determine throughput. Robust figures for the total effect are lacking, but a larger vehicle does not automatically carry more people while potentially demanding more physical capacity. In dense urban traffic, narrow streets and parking, the effect is more immediate.

### Energetic travel

None of this is an argument against electrification. Over its life cycle, an electric car is generally substantially better for the climate than a comparable combustion-engine car. The IEA, for example, calculates that a large electric SUV produces about 20% more life-cycle greenhouse-gas emissions than a medium-sized electric car, yet still about 40% less than a medium-sized combustion car.[3]

This shows that powertrain and size are two different controls. Electrification makes sense, but even within the electric fleet it remains efficient not to move more vehicle than necessary.

The IEA compared electric SUVs with medium-sized electric cars offering similar range and found batteries to be about 25% larger on average in the SUVs. If every electric SUV sold worldwide in 2023 had instead been a medium-sized EV, approximately 60 GWh of battery capacity would not have been required. According to the IEA, that represents almost 6,000 tonnes of lithium, 30,000 tonnes of nickel, nearly 7,000 tonnes of cobalt and more than 8,000 tonnes of manganese.

The same principle applies to charging infrastructure. A car that consumes more electricity per kilometre must charge more often at equal battery capacity. Manufacturers partly solve this with larger batteries, but then more kilowatt-hours must be delivered per vehicle. At busy fast-charging sites, capacity depends not only on the number of chargers but also on how much energy each passing car requires.

Technological progress and vehicle size can consequently counteract one another. We can build more efficient powertrains, better batteries and more charging capacity while using part of the gain to move more car.

### The functional question

This leads to a habit that has become almost self-evident in car ownership: we size a permanent possession for exceptional use.

A car may carry one or two people for most of the year and still be purchased for the two weeks in which a family drives to France with luggage. The extra space, mass and dimensions are then carried during the other fifty weeks.

We do not apply this reasoning as readily to other objects. We do not buy a removal van because we move once every five years. Occasional car capacity can likewise be supplied by a roof box, trailer, rental or shared car, van or camper.

That requires a fair financial comparison. A €1,000 rental car feels expensive when the alternative is a car already parked outside. Economically, however, it should be compared with the **annual additional cost of permanently owning and driving the larger vehicle**. If a compact car costs €150 less per month all-in, €1,800 is available every year before the larger car becomes financially preferable. The result varies greatly with vehicle, mileage, depreciation, insurance and rental period, but the comparison is worth making.

Car sharing extends the idea. Someone with access to several vehicles does not need to own a universal car: a small car for ordinary journeys, an estate for a weekend away and a van for moving house may be more efficient than driving every day in a vehicle designed for the largest imaginable exception.

We may therefore begin car purchasing one question too late. Instead of first asking which car we want, we could first determine **which mobility we need**. How often is a car required? How many people and belongings normally travel? Where is it parked? Is towing capacity routinely necessary? Is easy entry functionally important? How many kilometres are driven, and which requirements apply only a few days a year?

The answer need not be a small car. For someone who carries a family, tools or substantial luggage every day, a large car may be the efficient solution. For someone else, a compact private car plus occasional rental may be more rational; for a city resident, perhaps cycling, public transport and shared cars. The relevant principle is not *small*, but **appropriate**.

### Shared responsibility

Consumers cannot solve this issue alone. Cars are developed for international markets, and the Netherlands is too small to change vehicle platforms by itself. Requirements concerning vehicle design, protection of cyclists and pedestrians, direct vision and front geometry require at least European action; international harmonisation makes sense for some technical standards.

That does not mean the Netherlands or individual buyers can do nothing in the meantime. The country can examine fiscal incentives tied to mass. Municipalities can ask whether vehicles with very different spatial footprints should use scarce public parking at the same price. Manufacturers can continue developing attractive compact models, and consumers can treat size as a criterion alongside price, range, power and equipment.

Cross-border problems readily create administrative paralysis: if Europe ultimately has to act, waiting locally can appear rational. Yet consumers, municipalities, national governments, European legislators and manufacturers influence one another and can move simultaneously. The inability to solve a problem completely at one level does not make measures at other levels pointless.

There is a personal responsibility too. Someone who genuinely needs a larger car has a good reason. But because some costs of additional mass and dimensions fall outside the vehicle, it seems reasonable to ask not only what we can afford and enjoy, but which additional function we are actually buying. The extra width stands in the street, the extra mass participates in a collision and the higher front also faces cyclists and pedestrians.

### Conclusion

The car industry has achieved enormous technical progress: engines and electric powertrains are more efficient, batteries have improved, bodies are safer and aerodynamics more refined. Yet part of that progress is being used to make cars larger, taller, wider, heavier and more powerful. In a compact country with roughly 18 million inhabitants and 9.5 million cars, the shared costs can quickly become substantial. Lubach's diagnosis of car obesity therefore remains relevant.

Democratic government requires anticipation, and democracy belongs to all of us. Responsibility can be exercised wherever decisions are made: by buyers, manufacturers and public authorities alike.

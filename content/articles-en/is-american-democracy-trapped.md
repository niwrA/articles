For much of the postwar era, the United States could reasonably present itself as one of the world's leading liberal democracies. It was never a perfect democracy, and some features of its political system have always sat uncomfortably with the principle of equal political representation. But its democratic institutions were stable, its civil liberties extensive, and its constitutional system appeared remarkably durable. Something has changed. There is no single objective instrument with which to measure the quality of a democracy. Nevertheless, several organizations that have attempted to do so over long periods now independently report a significant deterioration in the United States.

Freedom House scored the United States at 93 out of 100 in 2005. In its 2026 report, assessing conditions during 2025, that had fallen to 81. The Economist Intelligence Unit's Democracy Index declined from 8.22 in 2006 to 7.65 in 2025, leaving the United States in the category it calls a "flawed democracy." The Varieties of Democracy Institute, or V-Dem, shows an even more dramatic recent decline: its Liberal Democracy Index for the United States fell from 0.79 in 2023 to 0.57 in 2025. V-Dem now classifies the United States as an "electoral democracy" rather than a "liberal democracy."[1–3]

These indices use different methodologies, and their scores should not be mistaken for physical measurements. Judgements about judicial independence, executive restraint or freedom of the press inevitably contain interpretative elements. The magnitude of V-Dem's latest downgrade in particular can reasonably be debated. The direction of travel is harder to dismiss. Three independently constructed indices, measuring somewhat different aspects of democracy, all show deterioration. V-Dem now scores the United States below every other G7 democracy. Pew Research Center found in 2026 that 69 percent of Americans themselves were dissatisfied with the way their democracy was working.[4]

The immediate explanations matter. V-Dem points particularly to deteriorating legislative and judicial constraints on executive power. Other assessments identify pressures involving political rights, civil liberties and institutional checks. These are developments involving particular political actors and institutions, not automatic consequences of an electoral system. But they raise a deeper question. Why has the American political system proved so vulnerable to this process?

## The Netherlands as a first comparison

Consider the Netherlands. The comparison is imperfect. The Netherlands is much smaller. It is a parliamentary rather than presidential democracy, has a different political history, and exists within the institutional framework of the European Union. Dutch politics has its own serious problems: polarization, declining trust, difficult coalition formation, immigration conflicts and governments that can take months to construct. Nevertheless, on measures of liberal democracy it continues to perform very well. Freedom House currently scores the Netherlands at 97 out of 100, compared with 81 for the United States.[1][5] One of the most conspicuous differences is how the two countries turn votes into political representation.

The Netherlands elects the 150 members of its lower house through an unusually proportional system. There are many parties because a political movement does not have to become one of the two largest movements in the country—or even in a particular geographical district—to gain representation.[6] The United States operates very differently. Almost every important American election ultimately asks voters to choose a single winner. House members represent single-member districts. Senators are elected individually. The presidency has exactly one winner. This has consequences far beyond the design of the ballot.

## Two comparisons that sharpen the question

The Netherlands and the United States differ in too many respects for their electoral systems to explain the contrast by themselves. Two additional comparisons help separate scale, constitutional structure and electoral design.

### The Netherlands and California

California is not a sovereign country, but it is a useful intermediate case. Its population of roughly 39 million is more than twice that of the Netherlands, whose population is just over 18 million.[15][16] California also governs consequential areas including taxation, education, infrastructure, environmental policy and much of criminal and civil law. Size alone therefore does not make comparison with the Netherlands meaningless. Institutionally, however, California looks much more like the broader American pattern. Its legislature contains 80 Assembly members and 40 senators, all representing individual districts.[17] For state and congressional offices, its top-two primary sends only the two highest-placed candidates to the general election, even when both express the same party preference.[18]

This arrangement can make competition *within* the two major coalitions more open than a closed partisan primary. It does much less to translate support for several political movements into representation. A party with ten percent spread across California still has no obvious route to ten percent of the legislature. California consequently weakens one easy objection to the Dutch comparison. The relevant difference is not merely that the Netherlands is small. A polity substantially larger than the Netherlands can still retain the same single-winner logic and the same high barrier to multiparty representation. At the same time, California cannot test every national alternative: federal law requires single-member districts for the US House, and the state remains embedded in the federal party system.

### The United States and the European Union

The European Union provides an almost inverse comparison. With about 451 million residents, it operates at a continental scale larger than the United States.[19] Its 720-member Parliament is elected within the member states through systems that must use proportional representation. Members subsequently organize into several cross-national political groups.[20] This demonstrates that scale by itself does not require electoral competition to collapse into two parties. It does **not** demonstrate that the EU has solved the American problem. The EU is not a sovereign federal state equivalent to the United States. Legislative and executive authority is distributed among the directly elected Parliament, the Council representing national governments and the European Commission. Ordinary legislation generally requires agreement between institutions, while some sensitive fields retain unanimity and therefore a national veto.[21][22] Citizens can consequently find it difficult to determine which institution or government is responsible for a decision. Representation is also deliberately degressively proportional: citizens of smaller member states receive more parliamentary representation per person than citizens of larger ones.[20]

The EU comparison therefore cuts both ways. It shows that a very large, diverse political system can sustain multiparty representation. It also shows the costs that can accompany dispersed authority: complex accountability, indirect executive selection, slow bargaining and veto points. The institutional question is not whether plurality is costless, but which combination of representation, decisiveness and accountability produces the least damaging failure modes.

## Why America has two parties

There is nothing natural about dividing political thought into exactly two categories. Among American voters are democratic socialists, progressives, social liberals, centrists, libertarians, traditional conservatives, religious conservatives, populists and many people who fit none of those descriptions particularly well. Yet almost all politically consequential national activity eventually becomes organized around two institutions: the Democratic and Republican parties. Political science offers a well-established explanation for much of this. Single-member plurality elections tend to generate two-party competition, an observation usually associated with Duverger's law. It is a tendency rather than an inviolable law—there are important exceptions—but the mechanism is straightforward and has considerable empirical support.[7]

Imagine an election in which the preferences are:

- Republican: 42 percent
- Democrat: 38 percent
- New progressive party: 20 percent

If most supporters of the new party would prefer the Democrat to the Republican, their new party has created an unfortunate result. By voting for their favourite candidate, they may have helped elect their least favourite one. Voters learn to anticipate this. Experiments on strategic voting show that when people face several candidates in plurality elections and receive information about which candidates are viable, tactical behaviour can rapidly reduce effective competition toward two alternatives.[8] Politicians face the same incentives. A talented political entrepreneur deciding between creating a new party and entering a Democratic or Republican primary has strong reasons to choose the latter. Donors have similar reasons not to finance candidates with little prospect of winning.

A two-party system consequently reproduces itself. American politics does not lack new movements, but those movements are generally encouraged to capture an existing party rather than compete alongside it. That distinction matters because internal conquest and external competition create different incentives.

## Politics as a discovery process

Proportional representation is usually defended in terms of fairness: if 10 percent of voters support a political movement, perhaps roughly 10 percent of parliament should represent it. There is another argument that receives less attention. A political system can also be understood as a mechanism for discovering political demand. The analogy with a competitive market is imperfect but useful. Competition does not merely give consumers more products to choose from. The possibility of entry gives outsiders an opportunity to discover needs that incumbents have neglected. Something similar can happen in politics.

A new party might organize itself around an emerging problem that established parties barely understand. It might represent a constituency that has been politically homeless. It might introduce new methods of developing policy. One can even imagine a future party built around radically different organizational principles: extensive computational modelling of policy, AI-assisted deliberation, systematic searches for policies that improve outcomes across traditionally opposing constituencies, or unusually rigorous commitments to measuring whether its policies actually work. Such an experiment might fail spectacularly. That is part of the point. In a contestable political system, a new organization can test an idea directly with voters. If almost nobody is interested, it disappears. If a few percent of voters support it, it gains a small institutional foothold. If support grows, so does its representation. Established parties must then decide whether to ignore it, imitate it or change their own positions.

The Netherlands offers a particularly interesting example in the Partij voor de Dieren, the Party for the Animals. A political party centred originally on animal welfare would face an obvious difficulty inside a two-party system. Animal welfare is unlikely to become the defining issue separating two enormous national coalitions. It can therefore remain permanently subordinate to taxation, immigration, healthcare, foreign policy and the many other issues around which those coalitions compete. A proportional system creates another possibility. The party can simply stand for election. It does not need to persuade an established party to make its issue a priority first. It needs to persuade voters.

Once such a party enters parliament, its significance need not be proportional to its chances of ever leading a government. It can introduce motions, participate in debates, generate media attention and—most importantly—demonstrate that some voters care enough about an issue to change their vote because of it. Established parties now have information they did not previously possess. They can respond. Political competition therefore occurs not only between alternative governments but between alternative ideas about **what politics should be discussing at all**. This is a form of political contestability.

## The American barrier to entry

Now imagine a new American political movement attracting 10 percent of voters nationwide. In almost any other domain, capturing 10 percent of a market would make an entrant formidable. In American national politics, it could produce almost no representation. If those voters are distributed relatively evenly across the country, the new party may finish third in congressional district after congressional district and win none of them. Worse, its success can actively harm its supporters' interests. A new movement drawing predominantly from Democratic voters can help Republicans win. A movement drawing predominantly from Republican voters can help Democrats.

The better the new party performs without actually winning, the greater the damage it can do to whichever established party is ideologically closest to it. American political innovation is therefore not suppressed altogether, but redirected. An insurgent movement has a more promising strategy: do not create a competitor to one of the major parties; take over part of the major party itself.

This has happened repeatedly in American political history. And here the consequences become more serious than simply offering voters fewer names on a ballot.

## Two parties become two coalitions

In a proportional parliamentary system, voters choose among several political organizations and coalition formation happens substantially *after* the election. In America, much of that coalition formation happens *before* the general election. Progressives, social liberals and moderates bargain within the Democratic Party. Populists, religious conservatives, libertarians and traditional conservatives bargain within the Republican Party. Primaries determine which factions gain influence. Eventually voters are offered two large preassembled political coalitions. This arrangement can work. Indeed, it has worked for a very long time. But something changes when the two coalitions become increasingly internally homogeneous and mutually antagonistic.

Political disagreements that could exist independently begin aligning along the same axis. Views about taxation increasingly predict views about climate policy, immigration, firearms, abortion, regulation, judges and eventually attitudes toward the president himself. Politics becomes increasingly one-dimensional: Democrat or Republican. At that point the two-party system begins interacting with a feature of American government designed long before the modern party system existed: the separation of powers.

## When parties cross constitutional boundaries

The American Constitution deliberately fragments political authority. Congress legislates and controls public spending. The president executes the laws. Courts interpret legal and constitutional constraints. The system assumes that these institutions will defend their own authority. If a president attempts to acquire powers belonging to Congress, members of Congress should have an institutional interest in resisting. But modern parties cut directly across those institutional boundaries. A Republican president and Republican members of Congress belong to the same political organization. The same applies to Democrats. Suppose a president asserts a novel executive authority.

The constitutional conflict ought to place the president against Congress. The political conflict can instead place the president's party against the opposition party. A legislator belonging to the president's party then faces conflicting incentives. Defending Congress may require weakening the leader of the legislator's own political coalition, while restricting presidential authority today may also restrict what the party hopes to accomplish while it controls the executive. This mechanism is not inherently Republican or Democratic. It is structural, and becomes stronger as political identity is organized more comprehensively around two opposing parties.

## The presidential feedback loop

This is where the structure becomes potentially self-reinforcing. Congress was deliberately designed to make legislation difficult. Major policy changes require agreement across institutions representing different constituencies and elected on different schedules. Strong polarization makes that process harder still. But political problems do not stop occurring because Congress cannot agree. Modern government has therefore delegated enormous amounts of discretion to executive agencies. Congress cannot itself specify every environmental standard, banking regulation, immigration procedure, drug-approval criterion or enforcement priority. It writes statutes within which executive institutions necessarily make thousands of consequential decisions.

When Congress cannot update legislation, presidents have an obvious incentive to ask how much can instead be achieved through authority the executive branch already possesses. Policy making begins migrating from legislation toward executive action, administrative interpretation and regulation. Courts increasingly become the institution that decides whether the executive has gone too far. A subsequent president can then reverse many of those decisions. This creates political whiplash: one administration interprets executive authority one way; its successor reverses direction; another successor reverses it again. And something important happens to elections. The presidency becomes more valuable.

If control of the presidency determines not merely who administers laws but increasingly which policies government pursues, losing a presidential election becomes much more consequential. The opposing party's president is no longer simply someone with whom Congress must negotiate for four years. That president controls an enormous administrative apparatus capable of affecting many areas of national life. Presidential elections therefore begin to feel existential. That strengthens partisan loyalty and increases the political cost of compromising with the opposing coalition. Which makes Congress less capable of legislating. Which encourages greater reliance on executive action.

That, in turn, makes the presidency still more important. The result is a feedback loop:

**two-party sorting → polarization → congressional gridlock → greater executive action → higher stakes attached to the presidency → stronger partisan conflict → more gridlock**

This does not prove that America's two-party electoral system caused its democratic deterioration. It suggests something subtler. The electoral system may make democratic deterioration harder to correct once it begins.

## What more parties would—and would not—change

Not necessarily. This is where comparisons with other presidential democracies are important. Multiparty presidential systems exist around the world. Some have strong presidents; some weak ones. Some are stable democracies; others are not. The older idea that presidents and multiparty legislatures inevitably produce paralysis has not survived comparative investigation particularly well. A major study covering democracies between 1946 and 1999 found that when presidents lacked legislative majorities, governing coalitions formed in more than half of those situations. Minority governments were not systematically less successful legislatively than majority coalitions, and coalition status itself did not predict democratic survival.[9]

Uruguay provides a useful illustration. Research there finds that presidents can construct meaningful legislative coalitions: parties receiving cabinet positions subsequently behave more supportively toward the government than ideology alone would predict.[10] More parties therefore do not make political self-interest disappear. They rearrange it. And that may be precisely their significance. Imagine an American Congress containing five substantial parties rather than two. A libertarian party might support a conservative president's tax policy while opposing an expansion of government surveillance. An environmental party might support a progressive president's climate legislation while opposing military intervention.

A centrist party might cooperate with either administration on fiscal policy while opposing attempts by either president to expand executive authority. Coalitions could comprise A, B and C on one issue; B, C and D on another; and A, D and E on a third. The constitutional importance of this is easy to overlook. The checks and balances of the American system do not ultimately depend on politicians being unusually virtuous, but on political actors having interests that conflict. A multiparty Congress could create more independent centres of political power and more cross-cutting interests. It could also create chaos, unstable coalitions, opportunistic micro-parties and new forms of extremism. There is no institutional arrangement that eliminates politics. The relevant question is whether America's current arrangement has become too effective at forcing almost every political conflict onto the same binary axis.

## Why electoral reform has to come first

Because that attempts to solve the problem in the wrong order. Under current electoral rules, a successful third party could initially make the system *less* representative by splitting one coalition and repeatedly electing the other. The problem is not primarily the absence of somebody willing to establish another party. It is the absence of an electoral environment in which another party can survive. The institutional reform therefore has to precede—or at least accompany—the party-system reform. Ranked-choice voting offers one partial solution. Instead of selecting one candidate, voters rank them. If their preferred candidate cannot win, their vote can transfer to another preference.

Maine and Alaska already use ranked-choice voting in statewide elections. Both were still using it in their 2026 elections.[11][12] That substantially reduces the spoiler problem. It does not, however, create proportional representation. A district still elects one person. A political movement supported by 15 percent of voters can therefore remain unrepresented. The more fundamental change would be to reconsider the single-member district itself.

## Five representatives instead of one

Imagine combining five congressional districts into a larger constituency electing five representatives. Voters could rank candidates using a system such as the Single Transferable Vote, or seats could be distributed through another proportional mechanism. A political movement would no longer have to become the largest political force somewhere in order to exist. If it attracted enough support for roughly one of five seats, it could gain representation.

That changes the political economics of entry. A new party could begin small. It could demonstrate whether its representatives were competent. It could introduce issues neglected by established parties. It could experiment with organizational methods. It could grow if voters approved of what it did and disappear if they did not. Political parties themselves would become more contestable. The interesting part is that introducing such experimentation does not necessarily require dismantling the American constitutional system.

## Reform within the existing constitutional framework

The United States has previously used multimember arrangements for House elections. The current requirement that states elect House members from single-member districts comes from federal statute, with the modern mandate taking effect after legislation enacted in 1967. It is not an explicit constitutional requirement.[14] That distinction matters enormously. It means that one can imagine substantial electoral reform without imagining the dissolution of the United States, a constitutional convention or fifty states simultaneously agreeing to redesign national government. States have even greater room to experiment with their own political institutions, subject to their constitutions and federal law.

This suggests a much less dramatic route. Some jurisdictions experiment with different voting systems. Researchers observe the consequences. Do more political movements emerge? Does representation improve? Do voters understand the system? Does polarization decline, increase or merely change form? Do legislators form workable coalitions? Does political participation change? Do new parties introduce issues that established parties subsequently adopt? Other states can watch. Successful arrangements can spread. Unsuccessful ones need not. This is already happening on a smaller scale with ranked-choice voting, although importantly there is no national consensus that the experiment is desirable. As of March 2026, eight states explicitly permitted or required ranked-choice voting in at least some circumstances, while nineteen prohibited it in some or all elections. Indiana and Ohio joined the prohibition side in 2026.[13]

American federalism is therefore doing something useful almost accidentally: different jurisdictions can test different answers. Proportional representation could eventually be tested in state legislatures and local governments without first committing the entire country to it. If those experiments failed, that would be important evidence. If they succeeded, something more interesting could happen.

## How change could become self-reinforcing

Suppose several states eventually developed genuinely multiparty politics. A new party might begin with a few seats in a state legislature. It could establish an organization, develop policy expertise and acquire experienced politicians. If voters liked what it did, it might grow. Other states might imitate the electoral system. Eventually political organizations could exist with substantial numbers of voters, elected officials, donors and governing experience—but with little ability to translate that support into representation in the US House because federal elections remained organized around single-member districts. At that point, changing federal electoral law would no longer be an abstract project advocated primarily by electoral-reform organizations.

Actual political parties would have an institutional interest in it. That is how reform can acquire its own constituency. And it is probably a more realistic mechanism for fundamental political change than expecting the two organizations that currently dominate national politics voluntarily to redesign the system in ways that make their own dominance less certain. Change need not begin in Washington. It may be more plausible precisely because it doesn't.

## What electoral reform can realistically achieve

None of this demonstrates that proportional representation would reverse America's democratic decline. The Netherlands performs very well on democracy indices, but its electoral system is only one of many differences between the two countries. Proportional democracies can elect authoritarian politicians. Multiparty systems can become polarized. Coalition governments can be unstable. Small parties can acquire influence disproportionate to their electoral support. Presidential power can grow in multiparty systems too. Electoral reform should therefore not be sold as a cure. What we can say with considerably greater confidence is narrower. America's electoral institutions strongly favour two dominant political coalitions. They make political entry extraordinarily difficult. They encourage voters to think strategically about which candidates can win. They encourage insurgent movements to capture existing parties rather than compete with them. And once political identity becomes highly sorted between those two parties, partisan loyalty can interfere with the institutional competition on which America's system of checks and balances depends.

At the same time, congressional paralysis increases the incentive to govern through executive authority, making the presidency more consequential and raising the stakes of the same binary political contest. Those mechanisms fit together disturbingly well. There is nevertheless an encouraging implication. If the diagnosis were simply that Americans had become irreconcilably divided into two political tribes, there would be little that institutional design could accomplish. But some portion of the division may be endogenous to the system itself. The rules reward two enormous coalitions, punish political competitors and repeatedly force heterogeneous political disagreements onto the same binary choice.

Rules can change. They need not change everywhere simultaneously. They need not be imposed on the basis of an untested theory. American states and municipalities can experiment, measure results, discard failures and copy successes. That process would be slow. A substantial transformation of the American party system might take decades. For changes this fundamental, that may be desirable. The objective should not be to engineer a particular political outcome or to decide which parties Americans ought to support. A more defensible objective is to make political power genuinely contestable: to allow new organizations, new ideas and new ways of doing politics to demonstrate whether voters actually want them.

The question, then, is not which third party America needs, but whether American democracy should make it possible for one to succeed.

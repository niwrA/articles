The Netherlands has a housing shortage. In 2026, the statistical shortage is approximately 4.8%, while policy focuses strongly on delivering roughly 100,000 homes a year. Without additional housing, a growing number of households will struggle to find suitable accommodation.[12]

At the same time, the Netherlands contains a great deal of living space. At the start of 2026, it had about 8.345 million dwellings with an average floor area of approximately 120 square metres. Together, they represent roughly one billion square metres of residential floor space. With just over 18 million inhabitants, that is about 55 square metres of physical housing stock per person.[1][2]

This does not mean every resident has 55 square metres. Distribution varies considerably by age, household type, dwelling type and region. It does show that housing need cannot be explained solely by an absolute shortage of square metres.

We will examine as many relevant factors as possible and consider how policy and practical measures might influence them. First, however, the problem needs a sharper definition.

## How much living space do we actually need?

Several forms of scarcity are often grouped under the same heading. There may be too little physical living space, too few self-contained dwellings, too few suitable homes in the right locations, or too few homes affordable to the households that need them. A good analysis should therefore count not only dwellings but also floor area, household formation and the extent to which the existing stock is usable by the population.

### Housing capacity

A dwelling is a clear administrative unit but says little about housing capacity. A 45-square-metre flat and a detached house exceeding 200 square metres each count as one dwelling. The same is true of a home occupied by one person and a home occupied by four.

At least three quantities therefore matter: the number of dwellings, total residential floor area and the number of people who can live there at a socially acceptable standard.

The last quantity is difficult to determine. A minimum below which health problems arise is different from the space people find comfortable. Privacy, working from home, children, hobbies, visitors and storage all have value. The objective should not be to use every home as intensively as possible, but to distinguish necessary housing quality from avoidable underuse.

### Household dilution

The Netherlands is gaining not only inhabitants but households. This distinction matters because housing demand relates more directly to household numbers than population. One person living alone needs a kitchen, bathroom, toilet, living room and entrance. Two people living together share those facilities.

A simple example shows the effect. Suppose a household requires 30 square metres of basic space plus 15 square metres per resident. A single person then needs 45 square metres, a couple 60, a three-person household 75 and a family of four 90.

Four people living as one household use 90 square metres in this example. The same four people divided among four single-person households use 180 square metres. Population remains unchanged while required living space doubles.

CBS projected approximately 8.47 million private households in 2025. Its central projection rises to about 8.79 million in 2030 and 9.21 million in 2040—an increase of roughly 747,000 households between 2025 and 2040.[3][4]

Much of this growth consists of single-person households. Ageing is important, alongside separation, young adults leaving home, changing relationship patterns and people living independently for longer.

This may be described as household dilution: the same population is distributed among more households and consequently requires more independent facilities. This is not a judgement about how people ought to live, but a description of a physical effect.

Small changes have large consequences at national scale. With 18 million inhabitants, a shift from an average household size of 2.10 to 2.05 creates roughly 200,000 additional households. **Household dilution therefore operates on a scale comparable with several years of construction.**

### Possible policy levers

Government cannot and should not decide with whom people live. It can, however, address situations in which people would like to share space but refrain because of financial, legal or practical barriers.

A single person with two unused bedrooms could house one or two others. Whether that is attractive depends on taxation, tenancy law, municipal rules, mortgage conditions and possible effects on benefits.

This is a relatively fast policy option. New homes take years to build, while additional use of existing space may have an effect much sooner. Physical costs are low, although the potential is uncertain because behaviour plays a major role.

### Housing stock and household structure no longer align

Much of the Dutch housing stock was built for larger households than many current and future households. The country has millions of spacious family houses while one- and two-person households are growing.

A home that once suited a family with children may be occupied decades later by one or two people. That is not inherently a problem. A house is a home, and residents need not move because statistics call it spacious.

It becomes relevant when people would like to move but cannot find an attractive alternative. A home suitable for a family then remains occupied by a smaller household while demand for precisely that type of home persists.

It is often assumed too easily that a smaller flat is automatically a good alternative for an older resident of a large house.

### Downsizing is not only about square metres

For many older people, a home supports functions that disappear in a standard flat: a piano, studio, workbench, instruments, large book collection, guest room, storage or garden. For someone no longer working, such activities may constitute an important part of daily life. A 70- or 80-square-metre apartment may be structurally adequate yet functionally insufficient.

The relevant comparison is therefore not merely between 160 and 80 square metres, but between an existing way of life and the possibilities that remain after moving.

Not every function has to remain private. Many are used intermittently and can be shared. A residential complex might combine self-contained flats with a studio, music room, workshop, substantial storage, guest suites, shared garden, library or family room. Residents could use less private floor area without losing the same amount of functional space.

This distinction between **private floor area** and **functionally available space** matters to the design of new housing forms. If twenty households each require 60 square metres less private space, they save 1,200 square metres. Adding 250 square metres of shared facilities still leaves substantially lower net space use while preserving many functions.

### Location is part of housing quality

For older people, the surroundings matter as well as the dwelling. Someone who has lived in one neighbourhood for decades often has a social network, familiar services and established routines there. Friends, neighbours, a GP, shops, associations and family all contribute to the usability of a place. Research on *ageing in place* and social cohesion associates neighbourhood attachment and social networks with wellbeing and health in later life.

Moving a short distance is therefore different from moving to another town or region. At an advanced age, a large change in physical and social surroundings can be burdensome, particularly when routines and relationships disappear at the same time. Studies of forced relocation, disasters and displacement among older residents have found deterioration in mental health, physical functioning or social participation. That literature warrants caution about treating relocation as a purely logistical optimisation.

A suitable home twenty kilometres away may consequently not be a genuine substitute. For effective residential mobility, the same neighbourhood—or at least the immediate area—may matter more than housing models usually assume.

### Possible policy levers

Mobility may be encouraged more effectively by building locally for households already living in a district. Municipalities can identify neighbourhoods with many large homes occupied by small older households and add suitable alternatives nearby.

Those alternatives must offer more than the correct floor area. Shared hobby space, storage, guest accommodation, gardens and meeting rooms may distinguish a theoretically suitable flat from somewhere people actually want to move.

Financial friction matters too. Downsizing can increase monthly housing costs and entails moving, furnishing and adaptation expenses. Remaining put may therefore be financially rational. Incentives could help: part of the proceeds from selling a home might, for example, be reserved tax-free or with deferred taxation for future rent. Moving assistance, temporary storage and help sorting a house may also be relatively inexpensive if they initiate an entire chain of moves.

A suitable home for an older person does more than add one unit. It may release a family house, after which the incoming family vacates another dwelling. The relevant measure is therefore not only homes built but moves and mismatches resolved.[5][6][7]

### Subdividing homes

Part of the existing stock is large enough to be divided structurally. A 180-square-metre house can sometimes become two independent dwellings. Total floor area changes little, but the number of self-contained units increases.

This differs from house sharing: shared housing retains common facilities, while subdivision creates separate homes. Not every property is suitable. Fire safety, noise, parking, outdoor space and local infrastructure impose constraints, and location must match demand.

Subdivision is economically interesting because much of the physical structure already exists: land, foundations, façades, roofs and infrastructure need not be recreated. Clearer technical standards, faster permits and renovation finance could make it easier. Support is best tied to housing capacity actually added rather than expenditure alone.[8]

One useful measure is investment per additional dwelling-year. A €40,000 conversion producing one extra independent dwelling for twenty years costs €2,000 gross per additional dwelling-year, excluding maintenance and residual value. This permits comparison with other solutions.

### Adding floors and transforming buildings

Capacity can also be added to existing buildings by constructing additional floors or converting offices, shops and other premises. Land and some infrastructure already exist, potentially reducing lead time and spatial demand compared with complete area development.

The potential is limited. Not every structure can carry extra floors and not every office can be converted affordably. Uniform rules, clarity about technical requirements, faster procedures and active inventories of suitable buildings can nevertheless unlock capacity. Municipalities could map technical and spatial potential much more systematically.

### New construction remains necessary

Better use of the existing stock can reduce an important part of the problem but cannot absorb unlimited structural growth in population and households. New construction remains necessary. It creates genuinely new physical capacity and provides an opportunity to align the composition of the stock with future households.

Counting units alone is too limited. One hundred thousand small flats represent a different amount of space and serve different households from one hundred thousand larger homes. Their effects on mobility also differ. A dwelling suitable for an older person who releases a large family home may affect the rest of the market more than one that triggers almost no moving chain.

Construction should therefore be guided by a combination of unit numbers, net floor area added, type, location and expected residential mobility. Gross completions must also be distinguished from net stock growth because demolition and other withdrawals reduce the final increase. A useful long-term indicator is **net effective housing capacity added**, rather than new-build numbers alone.

### The long lead time of construction

New construction has a structural advantage but a major drawback: response time. Land acquisition, planning, procedures, finance, infrastructure, grid connections and building stand between recognising a shortage and completing homes.

Changes in demand cannot therefore be absorbed quickly through regular construction. Some homes needed in five or ten years must already be in preparation. Planning capacity is itself a relevant variable.

Some excess project capacity is rational because projects are delayed or cancelled and future demand is uncertain. Infrastructure, energy and drinking water must also enter housing planning earlier. Faster procedures help but do not shorten every stage. A robust approach requires both sufficient long-term planning and faster supplementary measures for unexpected demand.

### Population growth and migration

Housing demand is determined not only by household dilution and the existing stock, but also by population. Dutch population growth has increasingly been driven by migration. More people now die each year than are born, so current net growth comes entirely from migration.

Migration is not homogeneous. People arrive for work, family, study, asylum, temporary protection and other reasons. They stay for different lengths of time and form different households. Gross immigration alone is therefore not the relevant housing variable; net migration, duration of stay, household formation and housing form matter more.

A family of four does not require four homes. Four single people who remain in the Netherlands may eventually form four independent households.

### Labour migration and economic growth

Some migration relates directly or indirectly to the Dutch labour market. Businesses and institutions create labour demand. When the domestic workforce cannot supply it fully, demand for workers from abroad emerges.

The chain runs broadly from economic activity through labour demand and domestic labour shortages to labour migration, household formation and housing demand. Family migration may follow; a family migrant can fall into a different statistical category even when work initiated the migration chain.

Economic benefits and physical capacity should therefore be considered together. A company creating thousands of jobs may generate substantial value, but its workers also need housing, energy, transport, healthcare and other services. Some housing demand visible later is not unexpected at all, but a consequence of earlier economic expansion.

Migration is one variable, but several levers precede it. Labour-force participation and hours worked can rise, education can reduce shortages and automation can lower labour demand per unit of output. Economic structure matters as well. In a densely populated country with scarce housing, land, labour and energy, new activity should be assessed not only by jobs and value added but by its use of scarce capacity.

This does not imply that only activities with high financial output per employee are desirable. Healthcare, education and other necessary sectors have value not captured fully by commercial productivity. It does suggest placing economic growth, labour markets, migration, housing and infrastructure in the same capacity plan.

### Productivity

Labour productivity indirectly affects housing. If the same output can be produced with fewer workers, additional labour demand declines. Automation, digitalisation, robotics, education and better organisation can contribute. Where extra labour would otherwise be supplied through migration, higher productivity may also limit additional housing demand.

The effect is slow and indirect but potentially large over time. Investment in technology, skills, process improvement and labour-saving innovation can reduce labour per unit of output. Wages and working conditions also affect how much domestic labour becomes available.

### Vacancy

Long-term vacancy is inefficient in high-pressure areas, but administrative vacancy is not identical to immediately available space. Homes may be between occupants, under renovation, awaiting demolition or used without correct registration. Some frictional vacancy is also necessary for moves to occur.

The usable potential is therefore smaller than the total number of administratively unoccupied dwellings suggests. Long-term avoidable vacancy can be identified more effectively and, where appropriate, taxed or rented temporarily. This may add capacity at relatively low cost but cannot solve the national shortage by itself.

### Affordability

Physical capacity and affordability are different problems. Even with enough homes, households may struggle to pay for suitable accommodation. First-time buyers may earn too much for social housing and too little to buy, remaining in expensive rentals or with parents.

Additional purchasing power or borrowing capacity does not create a home in the short term. When supply barely responds, part of that purchasing power may be capitalised into higher prices. More supply remains the main structural answer to general physical scarcity. Social housing, targeted support and finance rules can improve access to existing capacity. Subsidies and expanded finance should always be assessed for how much benefit reaches households and how much becomes higher prices.

### Uncertainty

Relevant variables are not equally predictable. Ageing can be estimated relatively accurately because most people who will be old in fifteen years are already alive. Migration, interest rates and building costs can change quickly. Household projections consequently have wide margins: for 2040, plausible low and high scenarios differ by many hundreds of thousands of households.

Housing policy should not be built around one exact future number. A robust system needs several kinds of capacity. Regular construction provides the structural base. Subdivision, added floors and conversion can scale faster. House sharing, lodging and flexible accommodation can provide a short-term buffer. The value of a measure depends partly on how quickly it can be deployed.

### A better core indicator

The target of approximately 100,000 homes a year is simple and communicatively useful, but does not describe the whole market. If 100,000 units are added while others disappear and demand grows almost as fast, the shortage barely declines. Conversely, subdivision, mobility, conversion and sharing can add capacity without appearing fully in the new-build figure.

A more useful concept is therefore:

**net growth in effective housing capacity minus growth in effective housing demand.**

Supply includes construction, demolition, subdivision, additional floors, conversion and better use. Demand includes population development, household growth, household dilution, migration, ageing and changing preferences. Land, procedures, costs, interest rates, grid capacity, infrastructure and construction capacity constrain the speed of adaptation.

Such a model allows policy to change when an important variable changes instead of adhering for years to one previously selected building target.

### Conclusion

The Netherlands has about one billion square metres of residential floor area for over 18 million people and simultaneously a substantial housing shortage. This is paradoxical only if dwellings and living space are treated as the same thing.[1][12]

Demand is determined by population, household formation, preferences and the distribution of the existing stock. Part of the problem requires new physical capacity; another part can be reduced through better use of capacity already present.

Measures operate on different timescales. House sharing can have a relatively rapid effect. Subdivision and conversion take several years. Residential mobility requires suitable homes in the right places. Regular new construction remains necessary but has a long lead time.

For older people, mobility is not only about dwelling size. A good alternative must preserve sufficient functions and preferably remain within the existing social environment. A flat with shared hobby, guest and meeting space in the same neighbourhood may therefore be much more effective than an arbitrarily located smaller apartment.

Population growth should also be considered systemically. Migration increases housing demand but is partly connected to economic activity and labour demand. Economic expansion requiring additional workers also requires homes, energy, infrastructure and services. That capacity belongs in the same plan.[9][10][11]

The central policy question is not how many homes the Netherlands must build under all circumstances. It is the relationship between effective housing capacity and effective demand, and the speed with which that relationship can be adjusted.

This requires a combination of better use of existing homes, local mobility, subdivision and conversion, sufficient regular construction and flexible capacity for less predictable developments. Systematically monitoring these variables makes it possible to adjust policy as soon as reality diverges from the projection.

Pay later provides a real benefit: a buyer need not transfer money irrevocably before knowing that a product has arrived. Yet the same button can conceal a loan. Someone who sees €2,000 in an account while €700 in deferred payments is pending does not economically have €2,000 left to spend.

Two needs have become entangled. The money may already exist while the buyer wants delivery and payment to match safely. Or the money may be missing and future purchasing power is brought forward. Only the second situation is **credit**. This article therefore looks beyond the product label to what a buyer actually owns, reserves and borrows.

A pair of shoes may seem a small starting point for an essay about corporate governance and social harm. Yet both contain the same movement: a benefit appears in one place while risk or consequences become visible later, possibly somewhere else. The answer is not to compress everything into one moral score. We need two distinct but connected layers:

The first layer can be enforced relatively firmly: one euro cannot be reserved twice. The second remains partly normative: how much harm is acceptable, and who should be responsible for it? That is precisely why they should not be confused. Technical safety is necessary, but it is not yet moral justification.

## Part I — A safe financial system at personal scale

### One purchase, two economic actions

Suppose shoes cost €500 and the buyer has €2,000. A bank could reserve €500. The account balance remains visible, but only €1,500 is free. The shop has payment assurance and receives the funds after agreed proof of delivery. Economically, nobody needs to lend money.

If the buyer has only €200, those funds can be reserved and €300 requires financing. Only that shortfall should consume credit capacity. One user experience can therefore have two explicit sources instead of one opaque category.

This distinction is not merely theoretical. The Dutch Authority for the Financial Markets (AFM) says BNPL can reassure online buyers, but that its loan component also brings risks of debt normalisation and accumulation. It therefore encourages payment methods that offer assurance without a loan.[1]

### What the bill solves — and what it does not

The revised EU Consumer Credit Directive extends credit protection to services including buy now, pay later.[2] On 17 September 2026, the Dutch implementation bill was still before the House of Representatives; its legislative debate was scheduled for 28 September. The account below therefore describes the **bill**, not rules already settled in law.[7]

The proposal addresses important weaknesses. BNPL providers would in principle come under the Financial Supervision Act and AFM supervision. It introduces licensing or registration, fuller information and consent requirements, and conduct standards. Creditworthiness must be assessed in advance using relevant income and expenditure, proportionate to the nature, duration, value and risk of the credit. Credit databases such as BKR would be used. The bill also adds safeguards around remuneration incentives, early identification of payment difficulties and access to out-of-court dispute resolution.[8]

For minors, the proposal mainly repairs a gap between the rule and its enforcement. They were already generally barred from BNPL credit, and the industry's code already set a minimum age of eighteen. Yet for 2023 the AFM found almost 600,000 iDEAL transactions through accounts held by minors that could be linked to BNPL providers. They mainly concerned users aged 13 to 17 and averaged about €50. The AFM cautiously described these as possible unauthorised use and suspected that existing age checks were being circumvented. The Dutch implementation makes the prohibition explicit and requires effective age verification.[9] These figures cover the BNPL sector and cannot, without further evidence, be attributed solely to Klarna or any other individual provider.

These measures can reveal sooner that someone is borrowing too much or from several providers. The issue is material: four providers processed about 53 million transactions worth €5.1 billion in 2024. Among frequent users under 35, 65 per cent paid more than one BNPL provider within a month.[1]

As currently described, however, the proposal does not make a principled distinction between **delivery assurance backed by money already owned** and credit. Nor does it introduce shared real-time reservation of free capacity, a transaction state linking delivery to settlement, or a mechanism that treats only the genuine shortfall as debt. BKR records obligations and arrears, but it is not the transactional view of current free capacity explored here.[3] The bill therefore improves protection around credit; this model additionally asks whether some transactions currently offered as credit should instead be designed as reservations followed by conditional settlement.

### Free as long as everything happens on time

The price of deferred payment is not limited to the amount borrowed. Many BNPL transactions carry no interest while the customer pays within the agreed term. Missing a deadline can lead to costs and escalation. According to the AFM, BNPL providers issued approximately 6.9 million notices of default in 2024, charged reminder fees on 1.8 million transactions and transferred about 600,000 transactions to debt collectors.[14] At three major e-commerce platforms, almost 900,000 customers also paid reminder fees. This is therefore not a rare edge case.

Dutch consumer collection follows statutory steps. Before collection costs may be charged, the creditor must send at least one payment reminder and allow fourteen days to pay. Statutory collection costs then have a minimum of €40, and interest may also be due.[15] For a small original purchase, €40 can be a very large relative increase. The everyday word *fine* is understandable, but legally these may be reminder charges, interest and collection costs, each subject to different conditions.

This changes the design question. A credit limit indicates only how much principal someone might carry, not what happens after a missed date. A safer system therefore shows possible costs before purchase, sends timely reminders through a channel that actually reaches the customer, and offers a reasonable recovery path before charges accumulate. The AFM previously found that a simple SMS reminder reduced the proportion of customers paying late and incurring charges by about one fifth.[9] Arrears are therefore not solely a characteristic of the customer; they are also influenced by the design of reminders and recovery.

The interactive explorer shows this in simplified form. You can set a due date and payment date for each order. After the statutory cure period, the model adds collection costs. Under the current situation and the bill, these apply to the full credit amount; under the reservation model, only to the share that was actually borrowed. Interest and provider-specific charges are not yet included. This shows how two payment methods with the same purchase price can still produce very different outcomes.

### Minors, microtransactions and parental payment methods

Effective age verification for BNPL does not close a different risk. A child can spend many small amounts through a parent's account, a stored payment card, an app store or previously purchased game currency without entering a credit agreement. Legally, not every purchase automatically becomes the parent's debt. Minors usually need permission to enter contracts, except for purchases customary for their age; unusual purchases can often be reversed.[10] In practice, however, money may already have left the parent's account or payment method, after which consent and recovery must be disputed.

Microtransactions add a design risk. A purchase of a few euros appears limited, but repeated payments can accumulate. Virtual currencies and multiple exchanges may also obscure the real-money price. European consumer authorities therefore recommend showing prices in real currency, disabling real-money spending by default in games not exclusively intended for adults, and providing functional parental controls. They also stress that such controls do not replace providers' own responsibility.[11]

This concern is not theoretical. The Dutch consumer authority fined Epic over Fortnite practices that directly encouraged children to buy and used misleading timers to create pressure and scarcity.[12] For this article, the broader design principle is that a system should examine not only whether one payment is technically possible, but also what a sequence of small payments does, who genuinely consented and which behavioural incentives the provider deliberately introduced. Possible safeguards include a separate child budget, cumulative daily or monthly limits, renewed consent above a threshold and a clear real-money amount beside every virtual price.

### Credit cards show that reservation already exists

The proposed state model is not entirely new to credit cards. For a card payment, the merchant usually requests authorisation first. The issuer then reduces the available card limit through a temporary hold. When the merchant captures the payment, the hold becomes a posted transaction and ultimately an outstanding card balance. A cancellation may release the hold; non-delivery or fraud after capture may lead to a dispute or chargeback.

Three forms should remain distinct. A prepaid card uses money the user already owns and is economically similar to reserving owned funds. A credit card settled in full each month creates temporary credit collected on a fixed date. With a revolving credit card, debt can remain outstanding and accrue interest. The Dutch CCDII proposal also brings cards with monthly settlement further under credit rules, although it includes some exceptions intended to keep the product workable.[13]

A wider capacity ledger should therefore count a credit-card authorisation as used credit capacity from the moment it is made. The issuer's hold and the entry in the wider ledger are not two debts but two views of the same obligation. Capture changes only its state: *available* → *reserved* → *posted* → *repaid* or *disputed*. The addition to present card administration is that other relevant credit decisions could also take account of the current obligation, within clear privacy and access boundaries.

The present explorer still abstracts from this distinction. It allocates every purchase first to owned funds and then to one general pool of credit. It does not yet represent a payment method, a separate card limit, authorisation or monthly collection. A later extension could therefore let each transaction use owned funds, BNPL, a credit card, a revolving card or prepaid value. A hotel booking would be a useful example: the hotel temporarily blocks an amount while final settlement occurs later and may be for a different sum.

### A check is not enough: capacity must be reserved immediately

A register can show existing obligations. To stop the same room being promised twice, it must also handle concurrency. Imagine €500 of headroom and near-simultaneous applications for €400 from A and €400 from B. If both read the old state, each may act correctly in isolation while jointly creating €800.

A shared capacity register would use **CHECK → RESERVE → COMMIT**. Once A reserves €400, B sees €100. If A cancels, the reservation is released. No such shared system currently exists in this form. It would need testing and raises important questions about management, outages, disputes, privacy and exclusion. The example mainly shows why registration alone is not enough.

The same mechanism can handle owned funds as **RESERVE → DELIVER → SETTLE**. The buyer cannot spend the amount twice; the merchant knows payment is available; transfer follows an agreed state. Delivery is not a simple fact, of course. A scan can confirm the wrong parcel at the wrong door. A useful chain therefore needs states such as *authorised*, *reserved*, *shipped*, *delivered*, *accepted*, *disputed* and *settled*, with evidence and challenge rules proportionate to value and risk.

### Enforcement where state changes

The shop need not know why payment was approved. A payment provider can orchestrate, a bank or lender can assess capacity, independent infrastructure can maintain reservations, and delivery or dispute processes can provide evidence. Regulators would oversee conduct, resilience, competition and rights according to the eventual design.

A useful principle follows: **place enforcement where state changes, but oversight where incentives are independent**. A party paid for increasing transactions should not decide acceptable risk without countervailing control. Centralisation is not automatically better either. A design must separate information, disclose only the necessary answer and provide repair when data or decisions are wrong.

Assessment can change slowly while commitments change quickly. A regulated party might periodically set a ceiling; the ledger records only how much has been committed. A voluntary personal limit can be below the permitted maximum. Owned funds remain usable even when no new loan fits.

## Part II — A moral and governance system at societal scale

### A safe transaction can still be badly aligned

Financial capacity does not answer who benefits and who carries the consequences. A business can easily fund a project while pollution falls on neighbours. An executive can be rewarded for profit now while maintenance risk or illness appears after departure. A derivative can hedge an existing exposure, but it can also create a powerful payoff from damage its holder can influence.

We therefore need a second overview—not a central database, but a way to organise the questions. It keeps separate:

- **benefit:** who receives money, status or protection;
- **power:** who can influence the probability or scale of the outcome;
- **consequences:** who bears financial, physical, social or ecological loss;
- **accountability:** who must explain, repair or pay;
- **time:** when benefits and consequences become visible.

These variables should not disappear into one magic score. A low total might otherwise hide severe external harm behind an arbitrary weight. The explorer below therefore keeps its assumptions visible and places immediate incentives alongside delayed harm and safeguards.

### Hedging, speculation and perverse incentives

A put option is not morally suspect simply because it rises when a share falls. It may cap a risk the investor already carries. Derivatives can also create new liquidity needs when collateral must be posted; DNB therefore addresses stress testing, buffers and controls rather than nominal positions alone.[4] Short selling can bring information into prices and expose overvaluation. The better questions are: *what underlying position is being hedged, what influence does the party have on the outcome, and can its gain amplify harm to others?*

Compensation has the same structure. Salary, shares and options have different payoff curves. Options may reward upside without symmetric downside. Shares expose their holder to falls but can still favour short horizons if they can be sold before delayed consequences appear. Clawbacks, deferral and liability try to reconnect time and accountability; their force depends on detailed terms.

Externalities show the difference most clearly. If a decision produces €20 in private profit and €30 in harm elsewhere, its private result is positive while its social result is negative €10. This toy calculation does not prove how every effect should be monetised. It shows why accounts that follow only the decision-maker are structurally incomplete.

### What is a pay-later provider responsible for?

A lender is not automatically responsible for everything a customer buys with borrowed money. The customer, merchant and producer retain their own agency. Yet it would be too easy for the provider to present itself as a neutral payment pipe. It designs the choice architecture, determines acceptance and limits, profits from use and can observe aggregate patterns that no individual shop or consumer can see.

There is already a legal and governance foothold for this idea. The AFM describes product governance as a process in which financial firms must demonstrably consider target markets, performance in different scenarios, customer interests and periodic review. The more complex and consequential the product, the more thorough that work should be.[5] OECD responsible-business guidance adds a broader due-diligence principle: enterprises should identify and limit adverse impacts associated with their operations, products or services.[6]

This does not mean that PayLaterCo should morally approve every purchase in advance; that could become paternalistic, privacy-invasive and unworkable. A proportionate responsibility sits at product and system level instead: investigate whether the design encourages problematic stacking, impulsive use or concentration among vulnerable groups; publish relevant findings; change limits and interfaces; and permit independent scrutiny of conflicts between commercial incentives and customer interests. The question becomes not *should this one purchase have been prohibited?*, but *which foreseeable behavioural effects does this financial infrastructure create at scale, and what did the provider do when they became visible?*

### Two axes, not one verdict

The analysis ends with two independent questions:

1. **Safety:** does possible exposure fit financial and operational capacity?
2. **Alignment:** do benefit and decision power remain connected to consequences and accountability?

That yields four situations. An action may be reasonably safeguarded on both axes. It may be too large without a clear conflict of interest. It may be affordable while externalising harm. Or it may exceed capacity and detach responsibility simultaneously. The last category deserves early intervention, but the third does not disappear because a balance sheet is strong.

### What could limit or refute the proposal?

The idea does not improve by listing benefits alone. A pilot should compare whether a ledger actually reduces missed payments, double commitments and unnecessary borrowing more than simpler alternatives. Ordinary bank reservations, current credit registration and clearer balance displays may deliver most of the benefit at lower cost. Central state, false blocks or privacy risks may cause more harm than they prevent.

The alignment frame likewise needs to add something beyond accounting, supervision and liability. It could be tested by making advance predictions about decisions it will flag and comparing these with independent case analysis. If its categories can be bent after the event to support any desired conclusion, it is not a useful model.

## Conclusion

Many economic products simplify a transaction by hiding several functions behind one button. That is convenient only while obligations remain visible. Pay later shows what can be lost: owned money, delivery assurance and credit become one experience while displayed balance and real room diverge.

The proposed response is not a new omniscient institution. It is first a way of looking: reserve what has been promised, call only the shortfall credit, and ensure that concurrent decisions use the same current balance. Then ask a second question: who has benefit and power, who bears the consequences and who can still be held accountable later?

An economic system is not responsible because risk disappears. Risk rarely disappears; it moves. Good design shows **where it lands, who can influence it and who can still intervene when the outcome differs from what was hoped**.

The Netherlands has faced a tight labour market for years while its population has grown substantially. It passed 16 million residents in 2001 and reached 18.13 million by the end of 2025.

The source of that growth has changed. About 166,000 children were born in 2025 and more than 173,000 people died. Without migration, the population would have shrunk. Roughly 306,500 people immigrated and 211,900 emigrated. Net migration of almost 95,000 produced final growth of 87,200. Deaths have exceeded births every year since 2022.[1]

More people are working than ever: over 9.8 million people aged 15 to 74 were employed in 2025 and the employment rate was 73.2 per cent.[2] Yet in the second quarter of 2026 there were still 95 vacancies for every hundred unemployed people.[3]

That appears contradictory:

> **more residents → more workers → high participation → and still staff shortages**

The easy explanation is that the country simply still has too few workers. That misses a central part of the system.

## More people do not only mean more workers

Suppose 100,000 additional people live in the Netherlands tomorrow and 70,000 of them work. Labour supply clearly rises. Yet those people also need homes, buy products, use energy and transport and need healthcare and other services. Some have children who require childcare and schools.

Population growth therefore adds labour supply and creates labour demand at the same time:

> **net additional labour capacity = added labour supply − additional labour demand**

The two effects are not equal by definition. A full-time, single engineer has a different effect from a family with young children, an international student, a seasonal worker or someone who barely participates in paid employment. Age, skills, hours, household and duration of stay all matter.

## Migration is a flow

Immigration and population growth are not the same. Almost 307,000 people arrived in 2025 while about 212,000 left.[1] Even the balance is incomplete: somebody who works for four years and leaves has a different demographic effect from a permanent resident.

A useful analysis therefore starts with immigration minus emigration, then considers age, duration of stay, household formation, participation, hours and skills.

## Ageing works in two directions

As a larger share of the population reaches ages at which people tend to do less or no paid work, potential labour supply declines. At the same time demand rises, most visibly for healthcare:

> **ageing → less labour supply + more demand for care**

Migration can temporarily improve this relationship because migrants are often young adults when they arrive. But migrants age too. Labour migration can soften ageing; it cannot permanently abolish it. The State Commission on Demographic Developments 2050 therefore analyses migration together with participation, productivity, services and broad welfare.[5]

## Could existing residents simply work more?

The Netherlands already brings a large share of its population into paid employment, but many people work part time. Labour supply can therefore grow not only when more people enter employment, but also when those who want to do so work more hours. In the second quarter of 2026, 568,000 part-time workers wanted more hours and were immediately available for them.[3] The StatLine data also show how many additional hours they wanted on average.[4] This is not a labour reserve that can simply be activated, but it is concrete potential within the existing population.

### Paid work is not all work

If someone performs paid work for four days and cares for children or a parent for one day, the labour market records four days. Society receives five days of activity. An additional paid day may require childcare or professional care—and therefore workers.

Some unpaid work is then replaced by paid work. This does not make longer hours useless, but the socially relevant measure is effective capacity rather than paid hours alone. Nor is output always linear in hours: recovery, concentration, health, absence and the nature of the work all matter.

## Why do we need so many workers?

A vacancy means an employer wants to hire under current conditions. If nobody is available, the employer can raise pay, improve conditions, reorganise work, automate, raise prices, reduce or relocate production—or recruit abroad.

A staff shortage is therefore not an absolute shortage of human beings. It means that under the current technology, prices, wages, conditions and economic structure, demand for labour exceeds supply. Labour demand is not a law of nature and not every vacancy necessarily needs to be filled.

## Different economies require different amounts of labour

Two sectors may each produce €10 billion in value added while one employs 200,000 people and the other 20,000. They look equally large in output and fundamentally different in the labour market.

> **economic output = labour volume × labour productivity**

Productivity is therefore indirectly a demographic variable. An economy that grows primarily by adding workers requires more labour migration when domestic supply is insufficient than one that obtains the same growth from output per hour.

### Productivity is not social value

A nurse may have lower measured output per hour than an employee of a global software company. That does not make software more socially important. Healthcare, education, safety and government create value that market prices capture poorly. Economic productivity and social necessity must remain distinct. A broad-welfare approach also considers public services, housing and ecological effects.[6]

## Sectors use different scarce resources

Healthcare, construction, agriculture, logistics, hospitality, industry and high-tech use different combinations of labour, skills, land, energy, water, infrastructure, capital, housing and ecological capacity. Labour is not interchangeable either: training and mobility take time and resources.

### Agriculture makes the trade-off visible

Agricultural land covered 43.2 per cent of the Netherlands' total area in 2025.[8] The wider agricultural complex contributes substantially to output and employment but includes much more than primary agriculture.[9] Land, jobs and value added therefore cannot simply be divided into one another.

Shrinking intensive livestock farming also changes suppliers, processing and transport. Land and environmental capacity may be released, while exports of animal products fall and more of those products may be imported if consumption remains unchanged.

Yet the current livestock system itself relies heavily on imports. Maintaining high animal numbers requires large quantities of feed, and converting plant-based feed into meat, dairy or eggs inevitably loses part of its food energy and protein. The Netherlands can therefore be a major agricultural exporter in monetary terms while simultaneously being a net importer when measured in calories, protein and the use of foreign agricultural land. A recent WUR exploration estimates that the present Dutch food system uses about 4.7 million hectares abroad, in addition to roughly 1.6 million hectares of domestic agricultural land, mainly for imported food and animal feed.[10] A smaller livestock sector therefore need not increase total import dependence; the outcome also depends on what people continue to consume and what replaces domestic production.

Even when animal production moves abroad, that does not always amount to exporting precisely the same environmental problem.

CO₂ is global. Nitrogen deposition, odour, noise, water quality and ecosystem pressure are much more location-specific. Reduced Dutch production can lower local pressure while raising the global consumption footprint. Both can be true.

## The Netherlands has several limits

Human time, homes, land, grid capacity, water, infrastructure and nature are finite. A data centre uses little labour but substantial electricity. Healthcare is labour-intensive. Logistics uses workers, infrastructure and space. High-tech needs specific skills and often regional concentration.

The relevant question is not only how many jobs an activity provides, but what economic and social output it produces for all scarce resources it uses. If an activity contracts, labour, grid capacity, land or environmental capacity may create value elsewhere.

## Economic activity both funds and burdens public budgets

Sectors differ not only in labour, output and use of scarce resources. They also differ in the taxes and social contributions they generate and in the public expenditure they require. Turnover is not the same as value added in the Netherlands, and value added is not the same as tax revenue. Outcomes depend on wages, profits, ownership, effective tax rates, subsidies and where income is ultimately taxed.

Several public costs can stand opposite those revenues. An activity may require additional infrastructure, grid capacity, supervision, training or housing for workers. Subsidies, tax advantages, environmental harm and health costs may matter as well. Some costs arise immediately; others only become visible years later. A sector that pays substantial taxes may therefore still require a large public investment programme.

A highly simplified direct fiscal balance is:

> **public revenue − subsidies − running public costs − required public investment**

This balance is emphatically not a measure of social value. Healthcare, education, safety and public administration may directly consume more public funding than they remit as sectors, while remaining essential to people and to the rest of the economy. Conversely, a profitable commercial sector can combine a positive fiscal balance with substantial pressure on land, energy or the environment. These dimensions should remain visible beside one another rather than being collapsed into a single ranking.

### Required and affordable public capacity

Government and safety also involve two separate questions. Required capacity follows from population, regulation, infrastructure, crime, geopolitics and other social conditions. Affordable capacity depends on tax revenue, existing expenditure, debt and political choices. Additional revenue makes investment possible, but does not automatically determine whether the money goes to safety, healthcare, education, housing, nature restoration or debt reduction.

Time matters here too. Investment in the electricity grid uses money and scarce technical labour today but expands capacity only later. The same applies to housing, education and public services. The interactive model therefore expresses fiscal outcomes as indicative indices rather than euro forecasts. Users allocate investment capacity explicitly, while social need is calculated separately. This can reveal that a service is required but remains underfunded within the selected budget.

## Economic structure can influence migration

If the Netherlands expands a sector for which few suitable domestic workers exist, labour demand rises. Employers may respond through wages, training, automation or reduced output—but also through labour migration.

> **sector growth → labour demand → labour migration → population growth → housing and consumption demand → new labour demand**

Migration is therefore not merely an external quantity managed through migration rules. Part of it follows from the economy the country enables. The Advisory Council on Migration consequently points to labour-market and economic-structure policy as indirect forms of steering.[6]

Migration is not fully adjustable, however. EU/EEA and Swiss citizens enjoy freedom of movement; the Netherlands cannot set an ordinary national labour quota for them.[7] Labour migration from elsewhere, study, family, asylum and temporary protection each have different legal frameworks.

## Technology, productivity and less growth

When labour is scarce, society can look for more people or require less labour for the same output. Modular construction, robotics, software and process improvement can help, but not equally everywhere. A classroom of sixty is not necessarily twice as productive as one of thirty.

Available, inexpensive labour may also weaken the incentive for labour-saving investment. That does not mean migration always slows innovation—high-skilled migrants may enable it—but the relative price of labour and capital affects choices.

Growth can come from more workers, more hours or more production per hour. A fourth option is to accept slower growth or contraction in selected activities. Total GDP is not output per person, let alone broad welfare.

## Building capacity first requires capacity

Expanding capacity creates short-term scarcity itself. More homes reduce a later shortage but require builders, engineers, materials, land, electricity and infrastructure today. The same applies to healthcare, education and the grid. An investment can reduce a constraint in ten years while worsening labour scarcity during the next five.

## What kind of economy do we want?

The apparent contradiction disappears once labour is no longer treated as a detached stock of people. Migration affects supply and population. Population affects demand. Ageing affects supply and care. Sector growth affects vacancies; scarcity affects wages, technology and migration; almost all activity uses physical resources too.

There is therefore no self-contained answer to *how many migrants the Netherlands needs*. We first need to know: for which economy and population, with what productivity, hours, public services and physical and ecological limits?

A model can certainly help find more efficient combinations within selected objectives and constraints. This can prevent waste and produce substantial social and financial savings. But deciding which outcomes to optimise, which limits are firm and how to weigh income, health, leisure, nature and distribution against one another remains a political and social choice as well. A good model can clarify possible paths, consequences and trade-offs; it cannot independently determine which of them society ought to value most.

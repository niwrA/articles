## Why a mug sometimes wins, a disposable cup sometimes comes surprisingly close, and our behaviour decides the outcome

At the office, the choice appears straightforward. A paper cup feels more natural than plastic. A mug of your own feels better still: after all, you do not throw it away. But the question becomes less comfortable once we include the entire life cycle. A mug must be manufactured, transported and washed hundreds of times. A paper cup is rarely made of paper alone, because hot drinks generally require a plastic barrier coating. A plastic cup uses fossil resources, but can be extremely light. Recycling helps only if the cup is actually collected separately, is sufficiently clean and reaches a process that puts the material to further use.

The right question is therefore not: **which material is green?** It is: **under what conditions does one coffee-drinking occasion cause the least environmental impact?**

That distinction matters. A material does not have a fixed environmental score. Its impact emerges from a system: production, use, washing, energy, collection, recycling and disposal. Change one part and the ranking may change. That is precisely why this is a suitable subject for an interactive model.

> **Core idea:** the production impact of a reusable mug becomes smaller per use the longer it remains in service. Washing adds a new impact at every wash. With a disposable cup, almost the entire impact is incurred again for every new cup.

---

## First decide what we are comparing

A fair comparison begins with a *functional unit*: the same service that every alternative must provide. In this case, that might be:

> **One 180-millilitre serving of a hot drink in an office, including the cup and its cleaning or disposal.**

This avoids a common error. Comparing one ceramic mug with one paper cup tells us very little, because the mug is intended for repeated use. Its production must be allocated across all the occasions on which it is actually used. At the same time, washing must not be overlooked.

For a reusable mug, the impact per drink can be simplified as:

> **Mug impact = (production + transport + disposal) ÷ total uses + washing impact ÷ drinks per wash**

For a disposable cup, it becomes:

> **Disposable impact = production + transport + waste treatment − recycling benefit actually realised**

Here, total uses means the number of times the mug is used before it breaks, disappears or ends up unused in a cupboard. Drinks per wash is the number of drinks consumed from the same mug before it is cleaned. These two apparently innocent numbers exert a major influence on the result.

## Where does the environmental impact arise?

### The ceramic mug: an investment that must earn a return

Ceramics are made from mineral raw materials and fired at high temperatures. A ceramic mug therefore begins with a relatively high production impact. That is not necessarily a problem: prolonged use spreads this one-off impact across an increasing number of drinks. If a mug is used a thousand times, each drink carries only one-thousandth of its production impact.

A mug that breaks, is replaced or is left in a cupboard after twenty uses has had little opportunity to repay that investment. Its lifetime in calendar years is less relevant than the number of times it is actually used. A collection of twenty “sustainable” mugs of which only one is used at a time is materially different from one mug that serves for years.

### The dishwasher: small per cup, large as a system

In a reusable system, part of the burden shifts from production to use. A dishwasher requires electricity, water, detergent and equipment. The impact per mug then depends on four questions:

1. How much electricity and water does the selected programme consume?
2. How full is the machine when it runs?
3. How many drinks are consumed before the mug is washed?
4. What is the environmental impact of the electricity used at that time?

A machine consuming 0.8 kWh while cleaning 80 pieces of tableware allocates an average of 0.01 kWh to each piece. Run the same machine with 20 pieces and the figure becomes 0.04 kWh per piece: four times as much. This is not an exact life-cycle assessment—mugs do not all occupy the same space and other tableware also benefits from the wash—but it reveals the sensitivity of the outcome.

Timing can matter too. The carbon intensity of electricity varies with the current generation mix. Abundant solar or wind power may lower the emissions associated with an additional kilowatt-hour, whereas additional demand at another time may be met by a fossil-fuelled plant. A dynamic model can therefore use an adjustable emissions factor in grams of CO₂ equivalent per kWh. It should, however, state clearly whether this is an average or a marginal factor: these answer different questions and can produce different results.

Washing by hand is not inherently better either. Cleaning a mug beneath a continuously running hot tap can consume relatively large quantities of hot water. Washing it in a small amount of water, without paper towels and using efficiently heated water, may compare favourably. “Hand washing” is therefore not one process, but a collection of widely differing behaviours.

### The paper cup: paper plus a barrier

Paper can be made from renewable fibres, but hot drinks impose additional requirements. The cup must remain liquid-tight and therefore usually contains a plastic barrier layer. This combination makes processing more complicated than the word *paper* suggests. Production requires wood, water, energy and chemicals; the coating requires another material and may hinder recycling.

A paper cup *can* be recycled, but “recyclable” does not describe what actually happens. Separate collection, low contamination, suitable sorting and a buyer for the recovered material are all required. Cups containing drink residues in mixed waste do not spontaneously become feedstock. A Dutch office study also found substantial overlap between the results for paper, PLA and polystyrene cups once different realistic datasets and waste-treatment assumptions were considered. The researchers could therefore identify no universally superior disposable material.

### The plastic cup: light, fossil-based and dependent on the chain

A plastic cup may contain very little material. Its low weight limits production and transport impacts. Set against this are its reliance on fossil feedstocks, the risk of litter and the need to produce new material for every cup. Recycling can reduce the impact, but only where an effective collection chain exists. The theoretical share of material that is recyclable therefore matters less than the proportion of cups that is actually collected and processed into useful secondary material.

Biobased plastic does not automatically solve the problem. *Biobased* describes the origin of the carbon; *biodegradable* refers to decomposition under particular conditions; and *compostable* generally refers to specified industrial conditions. These properties are not interchangeable. A compostable cup that is not accepted by a treatment facility does not behave like a composted product in practice.

## What the science mainly shows: the outcome is conditional

Life-cycle assessments of cups do not consistently identify the same winner. That does not make the science useless. On the contrary, it reveals which variables are decisive and where categorical claims imply more certainty than the evidence supports.

A peer-reviewed study of Dutch office environments compared polystyrene, PLA, coated paper and reusable cups. The ranges produced by realistic assumptions were broad and overlapping. For reusable cups, the result was strongly influenced by washing behaviour and dishwasher loading. One robust and practical finding stood out: consuming two drinks from the same cup before washing approximately halved the washing-related contribution per drink. A third drink produced a further benefit, although the additional gain diminished thereafter.

This leads to a more important insight than “paper or plastic?”. **Avoiding a new product or an additional wash is often more powerful than optimising the material at the end of the chain.** Using the same disposable cup once more avoids a second cup. Using the same mug once more before running the dishwasher distributes one cleaning operation across two drinks. In both cases, behaviour changes the denominator directly.

Other LCA studies often find a break-even point after which reuse performs better than single use. But that point is not a constant of nature. It shifts with the material and weight of the mug, the disposable cup used for comparison, its lifetime, the electricity system, the washing method and the environmental category selected. A statement such as “a mug is better after 30 uses” is valid only under the assumptions of the study that calculated it.

## Climate is not the same as the environment

It is tempting to express everything in grams of CO₂ equivalent. That is useful, but incomplete. A life-cycle assessment may consider, among other things:

- climate change;
- energy and resource use;
- water consumption and local water scarcity;
- acidification and eutrophication;
- toxicity and particulate-matter formation;
- land use;
- waste, litter and microplastics.

One alternative may perform better on climate while requiring more water. Paper may seem attractive because it uses renewable feedstocks, but can be heavier and require substantial process water. Plastic may have a low production mass while still using fossil carbon and causing persistent pollution when it escapes into the environment. Ceramics can last a long time but require energy during both production and cleaning.

The interactive model should therefore present effects separately. A composite “environmental score” is defensible only if users can see the weighting behind it. Giving climate twice the weight of water use is a normative choice, not a physical calculation.

## Four scenarios that test our intuition

### Scenario 1: the ideal office mug

The same mug is used for years. Employees drink from it two or three times before it is washed. The dishwasher runs on an efficient programme and only when full. Production impact is spread widely and cleaning impact per drink remains low. In many climate calculations, reuse will build a clear advantage in this scenario.

### Scenario 2: the half-full dishwasher

Every employee places a mug in the machine after one drink. The dishwasher runs several times a day while half full. Washing impact per drink now rises substantially. The mug may still win, but its break-even point moves backwards and its advantage may disappear in some impact categories.

### Scenario 3: the collector of sustainable mugs

A newly printed mug is ordered for every event. Many are used a few times and then stored or discarded. The label *reusable* guarantees nothing: the potential lifetime is never realised. The model must therefore use actual use rather than technically possible longevity.

### Scenario 4: the well-organised disposable chain

The cup is light, used twice, collected separately and recycled with little contamination. This is a much better disposable scenario than a cup that enters mixed waste after one sip. It nevertheless remains a system that continually produces new cups and depends on consistently correct collection.

## What can an office do tomorrow?

The largest practical improvements do not require agreement on the perfect cup. They follow directly from the factors that dominate the result:

1. **Use what already exists.** Replacing an existing mug with a new “more sustainable” one causes new production impact.
2. **Drink from the same cup more than once before washing it.** Two or three drinks per wash can greatly reduce cleaning impact per drink, provided this is hygienically appropriate and users do not share cups.
3. **Run full dishwashers on an efficient programme.** Loading can matter at least as much as the efficiency label on the machine.
4. **Measure actual practice.** For one week, record the number of drinks, wash cycles, average load and disposable cups purchased. Without these data, you are optimising assumptions.
5. **Prevent loss and breakage.** A mug that lasts needs to repay its production impact only once.
6. **If disposables are necessary, design collection as a system.** Select the cup, bin, logistics and recycler together. A recycling symbol alone does not create a functioning waste stream.

Dutch regulation is also moving towards reuse. Under the changes announced at the end of 2025, reusable cups are due to become the standard in Dutch offices from 2027, while the recycling exemption for disposable cups in those settings will end. That is a policy choice intended to reduce single use; it does not replace analysis of the system as it actually operates.

## So which cup is most environmentally friendly?

When an existing mug is used for a long time, serves for several drinks before being washed and enters a full, efficient dishwasher, reuse is generally a strong strategy. But the word *reusable* does not exempt us from calculation. A heavy mug that is barely used and is washed separately with large amounts of hot water after every drink may perform worse than its green image suggests.

Nor is there a universal winner between paper and plastic disposable cups. Weight, coating, production energy, collection and treatment often matter more than the simple contrast between “natural” paper and “fossil” plastic. Recycling can help, but only for the proportion that is actually collected and processed correctly.

The most dependable conclusion is therefore both less satisfying and more useful:

> **The greenest cup is usually the cup that already exists, is used frequently, is not washed unnecessarily and forms part of an efficiently organised system.**

The interactive model does not turn that conclusion into a new dogma. It shows when it holds, when it does not, and which change would make the greatest difference in your office.

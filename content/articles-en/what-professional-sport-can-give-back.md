European football generated an estimated €40.2 billion in revenue in the 2024/25 season, excluding transfers.[1] At the same time, local clubs struggle to find coaches and volunteers, facilities need renovation and some children cannot afford fees, equipment or transport. That contrast readily becomes a question about whether elite athletes simply earn too much. Yet this framing misses how value is created. An exceptional athlete possesses scarce talent, has a short career and faces a real risk of injury. If millions of people specifically want to watch that athlete, it is reasonable for a substantial share of the resulting value to reach them.

The more interesting question therefore concerns the sporting ecosystem rather than one salary. Every professional began on a field, court or in a pool supported by clubs, parents, coaches, volunteers and often local government. Grassroots sport supplies participants, talent, supporters and culture; professional sport converts some of that into media rights, sponsorship and ticket sales. Could this chain include a feedback loop that returns part of the commercial value without damaging its engine?

I call that feedback loop a **sport dividend**. It is neither a finished policy proposal nor an argument for one rate. It is a thought experiment that exposes which evidence and assumptions are needed before any percentage becomes meaningful.

## A percentage also changes the market

If a sports market earns \(R_0\) each year and contributes a fraction \(d\), grassroots funding appears simple: \(G=dR_0\). Five per cent of €40.2 billion is just over €2 billion. But that calculation assumes that athletes, clubs, sponsors, investors and viewers do not respond. It may approximate a small contribution; it is almost certainly wrong at seventy per cent.

Players may choose another league, investors another market and viewers another form of entertainment. A loss of sporting quality may then reduce media and sponsorship income. The relevant equation becomes \(G(d)=dR(d)\): the rate rises while the base may shrink. Beyond some point, a further increase can raise less money.

We do not know the size of this response. No natural experiment has introduced different sport dividends across all relevant football markets. The model therefore uses a transparent scenario function with a tolerance threshold, market sensitivity and an accelerating response. These are not measured constants. Visitors can change them and can select a preset deliberately designed to break the proposal.

Sponsorship may work in the other direction. A demonstrable contribution to children's opportunities to play sport could make a club or competition more attractive to sponsors. This requires measured results; without scrutiny, it risks becoming little more than purpose marketing. The potential bonus in the model is therefore an assumption, not revenue booked in advance.

## Attention is itself a flow of money

So far, the choice appears to concern money earned at the top and subsequently distributed. Yet audiences, media attention and sponsorship need not remain in the same place. Suppose we collectively watched a few major clubs less often and attended more sport nearby: a leading youth side, local derby, athletics meeting, basketball evening or swimming competition. More than our time would move. Ticket and clubhouse revenue, local publicity and eventually sponsor value would move as well.

This scenario is fundamentally different from a levy. A sport dividend changes the destination of existing revenue through a rule. An attention shift changes where commercial value originates. Grassroots sport then becomes not only a recipient of funding but a producer of more income and audience value. The model therefore places both mechanisms alongside one another.

The shift is not costless. If ten per cent of attention leaves professional sport, ten per cent of the associated revenue does not automatically reach local clubs. A broadcasting right, international sponsorship contract and stadium ticket have a different value per viewer from attendance at a youth match. Some spending may disappear or move to other entertainment. The model consequently asks what share is actually recaptured locally and counts only that share as local commercial revenue.

### How can local sport remain enjoyable?

Local sport need not reconstruct the Premier League in miniature. Its distinctive value is proximity: spectators know players and parents, children see peers or older youth compete, volunteers meet neighbours, and the distance between field and audience is small. Recent community-sport research supports an association between the frequency and quality of social interaction and community attachment and trust, while also warning that these outcomes do not follow automatically from every sporting activity.[4]

An attractive local fixture therefore needs more than improved stands. A simple clubhouse, shelter, good lighting, accessible toilets and a safe cycle route can make attendance more pleasant. A regular match night, recognisable local competition, short preview, music, food and room to talk afterwards can create a recurring social occasion. At youth matches, the atmosphere should keep encouragement ahead of performance pressure; a larger audience must not turn young athletes into inexpensive entertainment.

Digital media can support local presence without replacing it. A shared calendar, brief highlights, results and stories about athletes and volunteers reveal what is happening nearby. A full professional broadcast is expensive and may encourage people to remain at home. A few well-chosen images may be enough to make them attend next time.

Sponsors receive a different proposition in such a system. An international brand primarily buys reach and association from a major club. A local business can combine visibility with relationships, employee involvement and a tangible project: lighting, an accessible viewing area, transport for junior members or coach education. Smaller sums can make a relatively large local difference. Dependence on a few businesses, commercial pressure on youth teams and unequal resources between affluent and poorer neighbourhoods remain real risks.

### A positive local feedback loop

If additional audiences produce income and better facilities, a positive loop can emerge:

> **more local attention → more revenue and volunteers → better fixtures and facilities → more local attention**

The loop has limits. Too many events can exhaust volunteers, disturb neighbours and displace ordinary training. Popularity may raise fees or prices, while a club with an affluent following may pull further ahead of one in a poorer district. Some locally generated money may therefore need to be shared again between clubs or sports.

Nor need an attention shift represent pure loss to elite sport. Local engagement may produce new participants and committed supporters. Major clubs can help develop regional competitions, coaches, combined ticket products and local media without reducing every relationship to talent extraction or brand promotion. Elite and grassroots sport would then do more than compete for the same attention; they would maintain different parts of one ecosystem.

The model represents this possibility without pretending to predict it. Its four local parameters are scenario assumptions; no reliable European figure establishes how much professional revenue would be recaptured locally after a broad audience shift. The policy question does become richer. Alongside “what percentage should we distribute?” sits another question: **which sporting life do we choose to make valuable through our own attention?**

## Two optima, no answer to one decimal place

The explorer marks two different maxima. The first is the rate that collects the largest net sum for grassroots sport under the selected assumptions. The second maximises a broader estimate combining professional sport, supporter enjoyment and effective grassroots investment. The two rates need not coincide.

Even the second maximum is not policy advice. Valuing health, social cohesion and supporter enjoyment is uncertain and partly normative. A result of 18.4 per cent would primarily demonstrate the numerical precision of the formula; our knowledge of reality is not precise to one decimal place. A robust analysis should instead identify the range across which moderate redistribution often helps and the assumptions under which that conclusion disappears.

Absorption also matters. A club cannot recruit unlimited coaches and a municipality cannot build sports halls without delay. Once the main bottlenecks have been addressed, the marginal return on the next euro falls. The model represents this with a saturation function. Money for which no effective use is available does not automatically constitute social value.

Crowding out deserves similar attention. If a new fund receives one billion euros while governments or existing sponsors reduce their contributions by the same amount, little new funding has appeared. Administration, project selection and oversight also cost money. Gross proceeds, net new finance and effectively spent funding are three different quantities.

## Where in the system is the contribution collected?

An athlete's income is not a single flow. Salary and performance bonuses come from collective sporting organisations; prize money, personal sponsorship, image rights, licensing and an athlete's own brand rest on different economic foundations. Someone concerned about concentration in a league's wage structure need not favour the same rate on an individual's image or sponsorship agreement.

There are also several bases outside athletes' income. A contribution could apply to total club revenue, media rights, transfer fees, wages above a threshold or personal commercial rights. Raising the same sum through these routes may cause different behavioural responses. The model's simple policy comparison uses illustrative relative distortion factors to demonstrate that principle; the displayed ordering has not been established empirically.

Football already contains solidarity mechanisms. UEFA distributes some club-competition revenue to teams that do not participate and earmarks such payments for youth development and local programmes. For the 2024–27 cycle, UEFA says the share for non-participating teams rose from four to seven per cent of the three men's competitions' cumulative gross revenue.[2] A sport dividend would therefore extend or redesign an existing principle rather than introduce an entirely alien one.

International coverage is crucial. A Dutch salary contribution can displace talent more easily than a scheme covering the major European leagues. Media rights and transfers may respond differently again. The question “how much?” may consequently matter less than “where, on which base and across which geography?”

## Public costs and solidarity are different questions

Professional sport also affects public budgets. In the Netherlands, police records for the 2024/25 season count 221,054 police hours across 827 professional matches.[3] The police caution that this does not capture all support and investigative capacity. Without a reliable comprehensive hourly cost, those hours cannot responsibly be converted into a national price tag. Maintaining public order is also a government duty, and demonstrations, festivals and other events require police capacity too.

A careful comparison therefore examines incremental public costs: which expenditure would not have occurred without the event? Against this stand taxes and genuinely additional visitor spending. Money that supporters would otherwise have spent in a restaurant or cinema should not all be counted as new economic activity. The net public position may be positive or negative; available national evidence does not justify choosing the sign in advance.

Internalising external costs and making a solidarity contribution are then separate choices. A club that pays its additional security costs has not thereby financed local sport. A club that invests heavily in youth development has not necessarily covered every public cost. Combining both debates obscures the problem a measure is intended to solve.

## What happens to the money?

A fund could invest in access, facilities, coaches, equipment, schools and inclusion. The same billion has a different effect in each category, and each has its own delivery capacity. A percentage retained within one sport also reproduces existing commercial inequalities: football can contribute a great deal while a small sport has little to distribute. Allocation across sports could assist disability sport, women's sport or less commercial disciplines, but introduces an explicitly political choice.

Effects also unfold over time. A better facility lasts for years; more participants may later become coaches, supporters or elite athletes. Strengthening the base may thus eventually support the professional market. The direction is plausible, while the magnitude is much harder to establish. Any model that includes this feedback should label it as an experimental relationship.

The same caution applies to health benefits. The average association between physical activity and health does not mean every additional euro automatically creates a fixed multiple in value. Reach, target population, activity and whether new provision actually causes more exercise determine the result. A social multiplier can help compare scenarios, but it is not an observed return.

## What should be measured first

The explorer separates one observation—European football revenue—from estimated and experimental parameters. Serious policy analysis particularly needs evidence on responses to different contribution bases, international mobility, sponsor valuations of verified impact, displacement of existing funding and the absorption capacity of sporting organisations. Career length, injury risk and existing pension, health and education provisions also matter when athletes bear part of the contribution.

Even then, one universal rate for all sports would be implausible. European football, tennis, Formula One and financially fragile Olympic sports have different revenue structures, careers and options for moving elsewhere. A sport dividend may be workable in football and produce little elsewhere.

This thought experiment therefore supplies no tax rate, but it does sharpen the question: **can professional sport finance more of its own foundations without disproportionately weakening the system that creates its value?** Under reasonable assumptions, the answer may sometimes be no. A model that permits that outcome is precisely what helps distinguish an attractive idea from sound policy.

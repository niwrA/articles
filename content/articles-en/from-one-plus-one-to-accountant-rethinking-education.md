## The question behind curriculum reform

Suppose we could redesign education in the Netherlands from first principles. Instead of adding subjects or adjusting examination rules, we would start with a more fundamental question: **what should a person learn to live an independent, connected, healthy, meaningful and productive life?** And how might we help them keep developing new possibilities long after they leave school?

Today's system has genuine strengths. Common standards protect opportunities; teachers provide continuity and community; qualifications offer recognizable signals. Yet education is still organized largely around years, subjects, institutional tracks and fixed pathways. People develop at different speeds and in different directions. The same student may be unusually skilled at 3D modelling, advanced in mathematics, beginning to learn Japanese and still developing independent study habits. Why compress such a profile into one educational level?

Another student may struggle in a traditional classroom yet flourish while learning a practical trade. That does not establish that school should be abandoned. It does suggest that classroom attendance, learning, demonstrated competence and qualification should not be treated as identical concepts.

The proposal is **a guaranteed common foundation combined with many possible routes toward further development**. It is not a call to abolish schools, replace teachers with AI or force children into early career decisions.

## A common foundation without a single path

Everyone needs a reasonable foundation in literacy, numeracy, evidence-based reasoning, health, relationships, citizenship, digital competence and learning itself. These foundations matter beyond their immediate value in the labour market. The aim is to describe meaningful outcomes: not merely “attended a course on percentages,” but “can interpret interest rates, compare costs and evaluate numerical claims.” Dutch numeracy reference levels already describe aspects of functional competence and could provide part of the formal crosswalk.[1]

Beyond that shared foundation, learners should encounter a wide range of fields: art, history, science, philosophy, crafts, programming, cooking, music and languages. They should also be free to develop serious specializations. Choosing Japanese over a conventional language option, or spending substantial time on robotics or hairdressing, need not be the opposite of broad education. Authentic projects can create reasons to learn mathematics, communication, technology and economics.

However, flexibility must not become abandonment. Young learners need expert guidance, stable communities, safety, access to facilities and protection against inequalities in family resources. A personalized path is not necessarily a solitary path.

## From a skill tree to a capability graph

Advanced computer games often display a *skill tree*: new abilities unlock additional possibilities. Real learning calls for something less rigid: a **capability graph**. A skill can contribute to several occupations or aspects of everyday life. Quantitative reasoning supports personal finance, engineering and accounting. Communication connects lawyers, architects, hairdressers and healthcare workers. Skills combine, branch and sometimes develop in parallel.

Each node should say precisely what a person can do, under what conditions, with what tools and to what standard. Relationships need types. Some prerequisites are genuinely necessary; others are merely a useful order for instruction. Some capabilities support entirely different branches, and some outcomes can be reached through several alternative learning routes.

The learner could then ask: “What would it take to become an architect?”, “What if I wanted to qualify as a lawyer?”, “What can I do with Japanese plus industrial automation?”, or “Which adjacent profession could I enter after modest additional learning?” The underlying profile remains unchanged; only the goal changes.

This graph should include life, social and civic capabilities as well as occupational skills. Not because every human quality can be assigned a trustworthy score, but because education should explicitly address more than examinable academic content.

## From 1 + 1 to accountant

Consider the trajectory from `1 + 1 = 2` to professional accounting. A child first develops number sense, counting and addition; later come place value, arithmetic, fractions, ratios and percentages. These abilities support everyday budgeting, interpreting data, understanding debt and comparing financial claims.

A person interested in business might move toward transactions, ledgers, reconciliation, financial statements and accounting software. Further specialization introduces accounting standards, law, risk, internal controls, assurance, ethics and professional judgment. Communication, data literacy and collaboration enter through other branches.

This is **not a compulsory, linear twenty-year course**. Running a fictional shop could teach a teenager why revenue differs from profit and profit from available cash. A financial administrator with years of experience might need evidence verification, not another beginner's course. Someone who later chooses engineering instead keeps the financial and mathematical skills already acquired.

Legal authorization remains separate. The Dutch AA and RA accountant titles are legally protected and require formal professional registration. A capability graph can show relevant skills and gaps, but cannot award a protected title merely by counting completed nodes.[2]

## The ability and the evidence are separate

A person may know how to use accounting software, but how should the system establish that? They might complete a digital practical test, be observed by an independent assessor, pass an institutional examination or present verified evidence of applying the skill at work.

The categories **online assessed**, **accredited**, **human observed** and **proven in practice** should not be treated as a simple ranking. Each offers different evidence and limitations. An excellent online simulation may demonstrate a narrow technical skill more convincingly than an unstructured interview. Years in a job may provide rich evidence, but a job title by itself does not prove every associated skill.

The record should therefore separate proficiency from evidence type and strength, including scope, date, issuer and uncertainty. Existing education, projects and employment could be translated into candidate skill profiles. AI could help extract and organize evidence, but **inferred from a CV** must remain different from **independently verified**.

## The other half: learning effectively

A capability graph defines a destination. It does not explain how somebody reaches it. That calls for a separate learning system connecting learners to explanations, worked examples, practice, peers, mentors, real projects, workshops and assessments.

The scientific evidence offers useful guidance. Retrieval practice and spacing can improve long-term retention. Beginners often need explicit guidance and worked examples; learners with strong prior knowledge may benefit from different forms of support. Metacognition and self-regulation—planning, monitoring and evaluating one's learning—can be explicitly taught within actual subject instruction. Structured peer tutoring can help consolidate understanding.[3][4][10]

That suggests a flexible cycle: explore a concept, practise with guidance, apply it independently, explain it to others, receive feedback, revisit it later and demonstrate transfer to a different problem. The order is not fixed. Someone already experienced may go straight to an unfamiliar practical task; another person may need a teacher, more examples or extra time.

**Learning how to learn becomes a central capability in its own right.** Can someone define a goal, find trustworthy information, plan realistic practice, recognize uncertainty, test understanding, ask for help, correct errors and retain knowledge? These abilities should develop throughout life rather than being confined to a short study-skills module.

Personalization must not depend on unsupported fixed “visual,” “auditory” or “kinesthetic” learner types. It should respond to prior knowledge, observed progress, accessibility needs, learner goals and evidence of what actually works.[11]

Collaboration matters here too. Explaining a concept to a peer can expose weaknesses in understanding. Yet knowing a subject is not automatically the same as teaching it well; mentoring, explanation and feedback are valuable skills of their own. Teachers remain important as subject experts, facilitators, mentors and assessors.

## Opportunities, demand and career changes

The same graph could connect capabilities with occupations, further training and regional or international opportunities. A warehouse worker might discover a relatively short path into purchasing or production coordination. A programmer might find adjacent work in education, healthcare technology or security. Learners could explore additional skills against estimated learning time, costs and likely opportunities.

Useful overlays would include current vacancies, projected demand, local constraints, wage information, mandatory qualifications and the scarcity of particular **combinations** of skills. The Netherlands' CompetentNL initiative, the European ESCO classification and Cedefop's labour-market forecasts already provide partial building blocks.[5][6][7]

But numbers require context. A vacancy count is not the same as the number of accessible jobs. Forecasts contain uncertainty. Rarity does not necessarily imply economic value. And someone who appears to possess 70 percent of a profession's mapped skills is not thereby “70 percent licensed.”

Most importantly, employment demand is just one outcome. Education must also support autonomy, relationships, curiosity, wellbeing and citizenship. The system should reveal possible directions, not dictate people's lives. Interest exploration could help, while optional personality-related information should never become a gatekeeper for children or adults.

## An open, learner-controlled infrastructure

The shared graph could be an open, versioned public knowledge resource. Individuals would control their own learning histories and decide which verified credentials to share. Schools, universities, professional bodies, employers, assessors and governments could contribute under transparent governance. AI might accelerate extraction, mapping and updates, but domain experts and independent institutions would have to validate definitions and consequential assessment decisions. European Digital Credentials for Learning already provide a practical precedent for verifiable learning records.[8]

A pilot must also respect current law. In the Netherlands, children aged five through fifteen are generally subject to compulsory education, and 16- and 17-year-olds without a starting qualification are generally subject to an additional qualification requirement, with statutory exceptions. A job at fourteen or fifteen does not normally replace school.[9] Initially, the proposed graph should map existing curriculum requirements and qualifications instead of pretending to supersede them.

## An interactive prototype—and the limits of the claim

To explore whether the architecture could work, we developed an offline **Capability Atlas** research prototype. It contains 151 candidate capabilities from elementary numeracy through financial and accountancy preparation, 294 draft relationships and three fictional learner profiles. Visitors can select destinations, inspect skills, change simulated evidence states and compare learning suggestions.

The first dependency model was too rigid. In a revision, **59 links were provisionally classified as proposed hard requirements** and **235 as recommended sequences**, with ten additional supporting links. All classifications are hypotheses awaiting expert review. The model also illustrates alternative learning routes for six capabilities and draft assessments for eight. It does **not** measure actual ability, certify anyone or provide live job-market estimates.

The demonstration establishes technical feasibility at a small scale, not educational effectiveness. The next step would be an expert-reviewed pilot: validate a limited set of skills, test alternative instructional routes against independent assessments, investigate retention and transfer, and measure fairness and reliability. Existing work experience should be tested against observed performance, not accepted without checking.

## Beyond the diploma

The most interesting question is not which badge someone receives. It is: *What have I genuinely learned? What can I do with it? What else could I become—and how could I learn that?*

That question matters to a child discovering `1 + 1`, a teenager struggling in a conventional classroom, a person switching careers and a professional in their fifties exploring a new field. A viable education system need not assume that everyone develops at one pace or stays on one route forever.

**Guarantee the foundations, recognize demonstrated competence, show people their opportunities, and help them become increasingly capable of learning independently.** That is a useful starting point for an education system able to evolve with society.
